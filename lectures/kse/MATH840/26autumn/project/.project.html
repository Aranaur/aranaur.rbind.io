ImportantThe brief

You work as a data scientist at an energy company. You have the hourly electricity generation of its portfolio, with the weather, up to midnight on 27 October 2026. The grid operator wants a forecast for the next 48 hours, hour by hour, with a 95% prediction interval: the interval tells them how much reserve to hold.

Everyone in the group forecasts the same data. Session 1 gives you 80 minutes. After that you have two days at home, and you may use any method you like.

weather_forecast.csv is what a weather service would have issued for those 48 hours. It is close to the weather that followed, but it has errors, and they grow from the first day to the second. You may use it or leave it out. Say which you did in the report, and why.

The template loads the data, defines the three metrics, scores the benchmark on a validation window, and writes and checks the submission file. There is no model in it. The submission cell starts from the benchmark, so if you run the notebook from top to bottom you have a valid file within five minutes. The rest of the session is for beating it.

The task

Forecast the 48 hours that follow the last hour of train.csv. For every hour, give a point forecast and a 95% prediction interval. You can use any method or library, from the course or outside it.

The submission file

The file is called SURNAME_project.csv and has 48 rows and four columns, in this order:

Column Content
ds the hour as YYYY-MM-DD HH:MM:SS, the same hours as in weather_forecast.csv
forecast the point forecast
lower_95 the lower bound of the 95% interval, 0 or above
upper_95 the upper bound

check_submission in the template tests all of this. We cannot score a file that fails it.

Scoring

Each forecast gets three scores on the 48 hidden hours. Lower is better for all three.

\[ \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{48}\sum_{t}(y_t - \hat y_t)^2} \qquad \text{MAPE} = \frac{100\%}{48}\sum_{t}\left|\frac{y_t - \hat y_t}{y_t}\right| \]

\[ \text{Winkler} = \frac{1}{48}\sum_t \Big[(U_t - L_t) + \tfrac{2}{\alpha}(L_t - y_t)_+ + \tfrac{2}{\alpha}(y_t - U_t)_+\Big], \qquad \alpha = 0.05 \]

The Winkler score adds the width of the interval to a penalty for every hour the actual value falls outside it. The penalty is 40 times the distance to the nearest bound, so a narrow interval that misses can cost more than a wide one.

The benchmark is a seasonal naive forecast with a daily season. Each hour of the next two days gets the value of the same hour on the last day of train.csv, and the interval is the standard one for seasonal naive. The template computes it for you. On the leaderboard it is listed as ----SNAIVE----.

Public and private leaderboards

The 48 hidden hours are split in two, as in a Kaggle competition. The first 24 hours make up the public part. On Wednesday and Thursday at 09:00 we score the latest file each of you has submitted on these hours and publish the table. The last 24 hours make up the private part, which stays hidden until Session 2, and the final ranking uses only these hours.

The split protects you from tuning your model to the leaderboard. If you could see your score on all 48 hours, every resubmission would teach your model something about the answer. The public table shows roughly where you stand, and the ranking is decided on hours nobody has seen.

The ranking on the private 24 hours combines the three metrics:

\[ \text{rank} = 0.3 \cdot \text{rank}_{\text{RMSE}} + 0.3 \cdot \text{rank}_{\text{MAPE}} + 0.4 \cdot \text{rank}_{\text{Winkler}} \]

We rank the better of your two submissions, the Session 1 file or the final one. If a change you made at home turns out worse on the private hours, you lose nothing. This rule replaces the condition in the syllabus that the final result must be no worse than the Session 1 result.

Beating the benchmark (8 points)

We compare your Session 1 file with the benchmark on all 48 hidden hours:

\[ \text{skill} = \frac{\text{RMSE of your forecast}}{\text{RMSE of the benchmark}} \]

Skill Points
below 1 (you beat the benchmark) 8
above 1, up to 1.1 (within 10% of it) 5
exactly 1 (the template's benchmark unchanged), or above 1.1 2

Final accuracy (5 points)

We take the better of your two submissions on the private 24 hours. A submission beats the benchmark there if it has a lower RMSE or a better combined rank than ----SNAIVE----; either one is enough. Everyone who beats the benchmark is ranked with the formula above. The top third of that list gets 5 points, the middle third 4 and the bottom third 3. A submission that does not beat the benchmark gets 1 point.

If only one of your two files beats the benchmark, that is the file we rank.

Methodology and diagnostics (8 points)

You decide which analyses to run. The report should answer four questions, worth 2 points each.

Question What to cover
What is in the series? The patterns you found, how the series changes over time, how the weather relates to it, and how all of that shaped your model.
How did you check the forecast before submitting it? How you compared your forecast with the benchmark on data you held back: which windows, how many, and what they showed.
How good is your interval? How you built the 95% interval and how you checked it on held-back data, with coverage and the Winkler score.
What is left, and what did not work? The residuals of your final model, and the approaches you tried and dropped, with the reasons.
ImportantHow the report is marked

Points follow the reasoning. A model that lost to the benchmark can earn the full 8 if you read the numbers correctly and your conclusion follows from them. An approach that failed is worth reporting: it tells us what you tried and what you learned. If you go beyond the obvious steps and can explain why, that counts in your favour.

Code quality and reproducibility (2 points)

One point if the repository runs from a clean start and produces your final file. One point for a README that explains how to run it and, if you used LLMs, how you used them.

Defence and presentation (4 points)

Two points for the talk, which should show how you approached the problem and how your forecast did against the benchmark. Two points for your answers to two or three questions about the project and about decisions you made in your labs.

Between the sessions

You can keep improving your forecast at home with any model, library or amount of tuning. LLMs are allowed; describe how you used them in the README.
