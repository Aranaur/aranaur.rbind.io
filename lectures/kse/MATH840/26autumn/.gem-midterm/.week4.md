Exponential smoothing keeps the components **and** updates them as data arrives.

## The one idea
Week 1 gave you two extremes:

## Average method — every observation matters equally

$$
\hat{y}_{T+h|T} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} y_t
$$

## Naïve method — only the last observation matters

$$
\hat{y}_{T+h|T} = y_T
$$

Neither is usually right. **Recent observations should matter more — but not exclusively.**
*How much more* is not a matter of taste: it is a parameter you estimate from the data.

4. **A taxonomy** — nine methods in one table, named by (Trend, Season).
5. **ETS models** — the same nine, wrapped in a probability model: 18 models, with intervals

6. **Automatic selection** — `AutoETS`, AICc, and why that is not the same as forecast accuracy.

# Simple exponential smoothing

## Simple exponential smoothing
## Forecast equation

$$
\hat{y}_{T+1|T} = \alpha y_T + \alpha(1-\alpha) y_{T-1} + \alpha(1-\alpha)^2 y_{T-2} + \cdots,
\qquad 0 \le \alpha \le 1
$$

- The weights $\alpha(1-\alpha)^j$ **decay geometrically** with the age of the observation.
- They sum to 1 (in the limit), so the forecast is a genuine weighted average.
- $\alpha \to 1$: only the last observation counts — the naïve method.
- $\alpha \to 0$: every observation counts almost equally — the average method.

The two Week 1 extremes are the two ends of one dial.

## The weights, drawn

## How fast do the weights die?
| Observation | α = 0.2 | α = 0.4 | α = 0.6 | α = 0.8 |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $y_T$ | 0.2000 | 0.4000 | 0.6000 | 0.8000 |
| $y_{T-1}$ | 0.1600 | 0.2400 | 0.2400 | 0.1600 |
| $y_{T-2}$ | 0.1280 | 0.1440 | 0.0960 | 0.0320 |
| $y_{T-3}$ | 0.1024 | 0.0864 | 0.0384 | 0.0064 |
| $y_{T-4}$ | 0.0819 | 0.0518 | 0.0154 | 0.0013 |
| Sum of the five | 0.672 | 0.922 | 0.990 | 1.000 |

- $\alpha = 0.8$: the last five observations **are** the forecast.
- $\alpha = 0.2$: five observations carry only two thirds of the weight — the series has a long memory.

## Component form
The weighted average is awkward to compute and impossible to extend. Rewrite it with a **state**:

## Component form

- Forecast equation: $\hat{y}_{t+h|t} = \ell_t$
- Smoothing equation: $\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1}$

- $\ell_t$ is the **level**: our current estimate of where the series is.
- One number carries the whole history. Each new observation nudges it.
- Forecasts are **flat**: $\hat{y}_{T+h|T} = \ell_T$ for every $h$. No trend, no season — by construction.

## Weighted form — iterate the smoothing equation

$$
\hat{y}_{T+h|T} = \sum_{j=0}^{T-1} \alpha(1-\alpha)^j y_{T-j} + (1-\alpha)^T \ell_0
$$

The initial level $\ell_0$ never disappears entirely — its weight is $(1-\alpha)^T$.

## Choosing α and ℓ₀
Both are **estimated**, exactly the way regression coefficients are: by minimising the sum of
squared one-step errors.

$$
\text{SSE} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left(y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1}\right)^2
= \sum_{t=1}^{T} e_t^2
$$

- Unlike regression, there is **no closed-form solution** — $\ell_t$ depends on $\alpha$ recursively.
- So it is numerical optimisation: pick $(\alpha, \ell_0)$, run the recursion, measure SSE, repeat.
- This is why "which $\alpha$ should I use?" is the wrong question. The data answers it.

## Example: Algerian exports
The optimiser chose $\hat{\alpha} \approx 0.84$ — a short memory: exports react to the present,
not to the 1960s.

## What α does to the fitted level

## What α does: read it
- $\alpha = 0.1$ — a smooth line that lags the series by years. It smooths away real movements.
- $\alpha = 0.9$ — the level chases every wiggle; the fitted value is almost the previous observation.
- $\hat{\alpha}$ — the compromise the SSE prefers.

## A smooth line is not a good forecast

A low $\alpha$ looks nicer on the plot and fits **worse**. Judge the level by its one-step errors,
not by how calm it looks.

## SES forecasts
## SES forecasts: read them

- The point forecast is a **horizontal line** — the current level, carried forward. That is all the
  model claims to know.
- The intervals **widen** with $h$: uncertainty accumulates, because each future step adds one more
  unknown error.
- Compare with `naive` from Week 1: also a flat line, but anchored on a single observation.
  SES is anchored on all of them.

# Methods with trend

## Holt's linear trend method
## Component form

- Forecast: $\hat{y}_{t+h|t} = \ell_t + h b_t$
- Level: $\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})$
- Trend: $b_t = \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta^*) b_{t-1}$

- Two smoothing parameters, $\alpha$ and $\beta^*$, both in $[0, 1]$; four things to estimate with
  $\ell_0$ and $b_0$.
- The **level** is a weighted average of $y_t$ and the one-step forecast $\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}$.
- The **slope** is a weighted average of the latest change in level and the previous slope.
- $\beta^*$ small: a stable, slowly turning slope. $\beta^*$ large: a slope that reacts to the last
  two observations.

## What β\* does

## The problem with a straight line
Holt's forecast is $\ell_T + h b_T$: the current slope, extended **forever**.

- Over $h = 4$ quarters that is often reasonable.
- Over $h = 40$ it says a company growing 5% a quarter today will grow 5% a quarter in ten years.
- Empirically, trends **flatten**. Unconstrained linear trends are one of the classic ways to
  produce a confidently absurd forecast.

Fix: let the slope decay.

## Damped trend method
## Component form

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{y}_{t+h|t} &= \ell_t + (\phi + \phi^2 + \cdots + \phi^h) b_t \\
\ell_t &= \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + \phi b_{t-1}) \\
b_t &= \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta^*)\phi b_{t-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

- Damping parameter $0 < \phi < 1$. With $\phi = 1$ this **is** Holt's method.
- Short horizons stay trended; long horizons flatten out to
  $\ell_T + \dfrac{\phi b_T}{1-\phi}$.
- In practice $\phi$ is restricted to $(0.8, 0.98)$: below 0.8 the damping is so aggressive the
  trend barely survives one step, and at 1 the numerics get unstable.

## What φ does
## Holt versus damped, estimated
## Holt versus damped: read it
- AICc prefers the **damped** model here — and the damped forecasts are *higher*, not lower, than
  Holt's. Damping constrains the slope's decay, it does not force pessimism: with $\phi$ fixed the
  optimiser is free to re-fit $\alpha$ and $\beta^*$, and it does.
- $\hat{\phi} = 0.98$, the top of the allowed range: this series shows no evidence of a flattening
  trend over 58 years, so the model damps as little as it is allowed to.
- The implied long-run level is worth computing — it is what the model believes about 2200.

## Two traps in `statsforecast`

`damped=None` (the default) lets `AutoETS` **choose** damping by AICc — so `AAN` with no `damped`
argument may silently return `ETS(A,Ad,N)`. And the `phi=` argument is a starting value, not a
constraint: pass `phi=0.9` and the fit still comes back with $\hat\phi = 0.97$. Fix $\phi$ in
`statsmodels`, compare specifications in `statsforecast`.

# Methods with seasonality

## Holt-Winters' additive method
## Component form

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{y}_{t+h|t} &= \ell_t + h b_t + s_{t+h-m(k+1)} \\
\ell_t &= \alpha(y_t - s_{t-m}) + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}) \\
b_t &= \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta^*) b_{t-1} \\
s_t &= \gamma(y_t - \ell_{t-1} - b_{t-1}) + (1-\gamma) s_{t-m}
\end{aligned}
$$

- $m$ is the seasonal period; $k$ is the integer part of $(h-1)/m$, which just means *use the
  estimate for this season from the most recent complete cycle*.
- Each observation now updates **three** states: level, slope and the season it belongs to.
- A season is refreshed once per cycle, so $\gamma$ acts on $m$-step-old information.
- Restrictions: $0 < \alpha, \beta^* < 1$ and $0 < \gamma < 1 - \alpha$.

## Where the γ restriction comes from
The seasonal component is often written against the level rather than the pre-level:

$$
s_t = \gamma^*(y_t - \ell_t) + (1-\gamma^*)s_{t-m}
$$

Substituting $\ell_t = \alpha(y_t - s_{t-m}) + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})$:

$$
s_t = \gamma^*(1-\alpha)(y_t - \ell_{t-1} - b_{t-1}) + \left[1 - \gamma^*(1-\alpha)\right]s_{t-m}
$$

So the two forms agree with $\gamma = \gamma^*(1-\alpha)$, and the natural restriction
$0 \le \gamma^* \le 1$ becomes $0 \le \gamma \le 1-\alpha$.

The level and the season compete for the same information. That is why their weights are linked.

## What γ does

## Holt-Winters' multiplicative method
## Component form

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{y}_{t+h|t} &= (\ell_t + h b_t)\, s_{t+h-m(k+1)} \\
\ell_t &= \alpha \frac{y_t}{s_{t-m}} + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}) \\
b_t &= \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta^*) b_{t-1} \\
s_t &= \gamma \frac{y_t}{\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}} + (1-\gamma) s_{t-m}
\end{aligned}
$$

- **Additive**: the season is an amount. Within a cycle $\sum s_i \approx 0$.
- **Multiplicative**: the season is a percentage. Within a cycle $\sum s_i \approx m$.
- This is the same question you answered in Lab 3 with Box-Cox: does the seasonal swing grow with
  the level? If it does, multiply.

Multiplicative seasonality is an alternative to transforming the data, not an addition to it.
Box-Cox **then** additive seasonality does the same job — and if you do both, you are modelling the
swing twice.

## Additive versus multiplicative
## The states behind the two fits
## The states: read them
- The **level** is the trend of the series with the season removed — the seasonally adjusted series
  from Lab 3, computed recursively instead of by STL.
- The **slope** is tiny and slow: Australian holiday travel shifts over decades, not quarters.
- The **season** is the interesting one. It is flat until the mid-2000s and then changes shape: the
  additive fit changes amplitude, the multiplicative fit changes it proportionally.
- The **residual** is what neither could explain. Additive residuals are in trips, multiplicative
  ones are relative — that is why the two columns are not on the same scale.

## Holt-Winters damped
$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{y}_{t+h|t} &= \left[\ell_t + (\phi + \phi^2 + \cdots + \phi^h) b_t\right] s_{t+h-m(k+1)} \\
\ell_t &= \alpha (y_t / s_{t-m}) + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + \phi b_{t-1}) \\
b_t &= \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta^*)\phi b_{t-1} \\
s_t &= \gamma \frac{y_t}{\ell_{t-1} + \phi b_{t-1}} + (1-\gamma)s_{t-m}
\end{aligned}
$$

This combination — multiplicative season, damped additive trend — is the workhorse specification for
business data with a strong weekly or monthly rhythm.

## Daily data: m = 7
## Daily data: what to notice
- $m = 7$, not 365: the cycle we are modelling is the **week**. Weekdays are high, weekends low.
- Two weeks of forecasts reproduce the weekly shape; the level drifts slightly and then damps.
- The intervals are wide on weekdays and narrow on weekends — with multiplicative errors,
  uncertainty scales with the level.

## Electricity has more than one season

Daily demand also has an annual cycle, and half-hourly demand has a daily one on top of the weekly.
ETS carries **one** seasonal component. Multiple seasonality needs the methods in Week 8.

# Innovations state space models

## Methods and models
## A method

An algorithm that returns **point forecasts**. Everything so far is a method.

## A model

A method **plus** a statement about the errors. It gives the same point forecasts, and in addition:

- prediction intervals that follow from something, rather than from a rule of thumb;
- a likelihood, so models can be compared with AICc;
- a way to simulate futures, not just draw a line.

Week 3 gave you residual diagnostics and told you the intervals assume something about the errors.
Here is where that assumption is written down.

## ETS(A,N,N): SES with additive errors
Start from the component form and look at the one-step error
$e_t = y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1} = y_t - \ell_{t-1}$:

## Error-correction form

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_t &= \ell_{t-1} + e_t \\
\ell_t &= \ell_{t-1} + \alpha(y_t - \ell_{t-1}) = \ell_{t-1} + \alpha e_t
\end{aligned}
$$

Now **assume** $e_t = \varepsilon_t \sim \text{NID}(0, \sigma^2)$ and the method becomes a model:

- Measurement equation: $y_t = \ell_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$
- State equation: $\ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + \alpha \varepsilon_t$

The level is not smoothed data any more — it is a **state** that the noise moves.

## ETS(M,N,N): the same method, relative errors
Suppose instead the error is proportional to the level —
$\varepsilon_t = \dfrac{y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1}}{\hat{y}_{t|t-1}} \sim \text{NID}(0, \sigma^2)$:

- Measurement equation: $y_t = \ell_{t-1}(1 + \varepsilon_t)$
- State equation: $\ell_t = \ell_{t-1}(1 + \alpha\varepsilon_t)$

With the **same parameters**, additive and multiplicative errors give **identical point forecasts**
and different prediction intervals. The choice of error type is a choice about uncertainty,
not about the forecast.

## The same forecast, different uncertainty
- Point forecasts agree to within a rounding error — the small gap is estimation, not structure:
  each model optimises its own likelihood, so $\hat\alpha$ differs slightly.
- The 80% interval is about a quarter narrower under multiplicative errors, because Algerian exports
  are a percentage that cannot go far below the current level.

## Holt's method as a model
## ETS(A,A,N) — additive errors, $\varepsilon_t = y_t - \ell_{t-1} - b_{t-1}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_t &= \ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t \\
\ell_t &= \ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1} + \alpha\varepsilon_t \\
b_t &= b_{t-1} + \beta\varepsilon_t
\end{aligned}
$$

## ETS(M,A,N) — multiplicative errors, $\varepsilon_t = \dfrac{y_t - (\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})}{\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_t &= (\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})(1 + \varepsilon_t) \\
\ell_t &= (\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})(1 + \alpha\varepsilon_t) \\
b_t &= b_{t-1} + \beta(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})\varepsilon_t
\end{aligned}
$$

In the models we write $\beta = \alpha\beta^*$ — the two parametrisations describe the same method.

Nine methods × two error types = 18 models. `AutoETS` searches this table for you; the notation
`ETS(M,A,M)` is how it reports the answer.

## The general form
Let $\mathbf{x}_t = (\ell_t, b_t, s_t, s_{t-1}, \ldots, s_{t-m+1})$ be the state vector and
$\varepsilon_t \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(0, \sigma^2)$:

$$
y_t = \underbrace{h(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})}_{\mu_t} + \underbrace{k(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\varepsilon_t}_{e_t}
\qquad\qquad
\mathbf{x}_t = f(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}) + g(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\varepsilon_t
$$

- Additive errors: $k(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = 1$, so $y_t = \mu_t + \varepsilon_t$.
- Multiplicative errors: $k(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mu_t$, so $y_t = \mu_t(1 + \varepsilon_t)$.

All 18 models are this pair of equations with different $h$, $k$, $f$, $g$. One estimation routine
covers every one of them — which is exactly why `AutoETS` can exist.

## Models that misbehave
- Some combinations divide by a state: **ETS(A,N,M)**, **ETS(A,A,M)**, **ETS(A,A$_d$,M)**. They are
  numerically unstable and normally excluded.
- **Multiplicative errors need strictly positive data.** With a zero in the series, the relative
  error is undefined and the fit fails outright:

For data with zeros or negative values, only the **six fully additive** models apply.

# Estimation and model selection

## Estimating an ETS model
Estimate the smoothing parameters $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \phi$ **and** the initial states
$\ell_0, b_0, s_0, s_{-1}, \ldots, s_{-m+1}$ by maximising the likelihood:

$$
L^*(\theta, \mathbf{x}_0) = T \log\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\varepsilon_t^2\right)
+ 2\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\left|k(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\right|
= -2\log(\text{Likelihood}) + \text{constant}
$$

- For **additive** errors, minimising $L^*$ is equivalent to minimising SSE — the same objective as
  the methods.
- For **multiplicative** errors it is **not**: the second term penalises states that make the error
  scale large.
- A monthly seasonal model estimates 3 parameters and 14 initial states. On five years of data that
  is a lot of freedom — which is what AICc is about to charge us for.

## Parameter restrictions
## Usual (traditional) region

- In the methods: $0 < \alpha, \beta^*, \gamma^*, \phi < 1$, so every equation is a weighted average.
- In the models $\beta = \alpha\beta^*$ and $\gamma = (1-\alpha)\gamma^*$, giving
  $0 < \alpha < 1$, $0 < \beta < \alpha$, $0 < \gamma < 1-\alpha$.
- $0.8 < \phi < 0.98$ to keep the optimiser away from the boundary.

## Admissible region

Wider: any parameters for which the distant past stops influencing current forecasts. For
ETS(A,N,N) the traditional region is $0 < \alpha < 1$, the admissible one $0 < \alpha < 2$.

An $\hat{\alpha}$ pinned at 0.9999, as in the Australian population fit, is a signal: the series
wants a random walk, and the level is doing nothing but copying the last observation.

## Model selection
$$
\text{AIC} = -2\log(L) + 2k
\qquad
\text{AIC}_c = \text{AIC} + \frac{2k(k+1)}{T-k-1}
\qquad
\text{BIC} = \text{AIC} + k\left[\log(T) - 2\right]
$$

where $k$ counts the parameters **and** the initial states.

- AICc is the default for ETS: with $m + 3$ things to estimate and short series, the small-sample
  correction matters.
- Likelihoods are comparable only on the **same data**: never compare an AICc from a transformed
  series with one from the raw series, or across different $m$.
- Lower is better, and a difference of less than about 2 is not a difference.

## AutoETS picks a model
For Australian holidays the search lands on **ETS(M,N,M)** — multiplicative errors, *no trend*, a
multiplicative season:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_t &= \ell_{t-1}s_{t-m}(1+\varepsilon_t) \\
\ell_t &= \ell_{t-1}(1+\alpha\varepsilon_t) \\
s_t &= s_{t-m}(1+\gamma\varepsilon_t)
\end{aligned}
$$

The estimated $\hat\gamma$ is at its lower bound: the seasonal shape is treated as fixed, and the
level absorbs the rest.

## What it rejected
- The three seasonal models are 130 AICc units better than the non-seasonal ones. On this series
  seasonality is not a modelling choice, it is the data.
- Among them the spread is under 2 units: **AICc cannot tell them apart.**

## The states of the chosen model
## Innovation residuals versus regular residuals
- **Regular residuals** $y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1}$ are in trips.
- **Innovation residuals** are the $\varepsilon_t$ of the model — here *relative*, because the errors
  are multiplicative.
- Week 3's diagnostics (ACF, Ljung-Box, histogram) belong on the **innovation** residuals: those are
  the ones the model claims are white noise.

# Forecasting with ETS

## Point forecasts
Iterate the state equations for $t = T+1, \ldots, T+h$ and set every future $\varepsilon_t = 0$.

## ETS(A,A,N)

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_{T+1} &= \ell_T + b_T + \varepsilon_{T+1} &\Rightarrow&& \hat{y}_{T+1|T} &= \ell_T + b_T \\
y_{T+2} &= (\ell_T + b_T + \alpha\varepsilon_{T+1}) + (b_T + \beta\varepsilon_{T+1}) + \varepsilon_{T+2}
&\Rightarrow&& \hat{y}_{T+2|T} &= \ell_T + 2b_T
\end{aligned}
$$

## ETS(M,A,N)

$$
y_{T+1} = (\ell_T + b_T)(1+\varepsilon_{T+1}) \Rightarrow \hat{y}_{T+1|T} = \ell_T + b_T,
\qquad \hat{y}_{T+2|T} = \ell_T + 2b_T
$$

Same point forecasts, as promised. The error type never touches the mean.

## Prediction intervals
$$
\hat{y}_{T+h|T} \pm c\,\sigma_h
$$

| Model | Forecast variance $\sigma_h^2$ |
| :--- | :--- |
| (A,N,N) | $\sigma^2\left[1 + \alpha^2(h-1)\right]$ |
| (A,A,N) | $\sigma^2\left[1 + (h-1)\left\{\alpha^2 + \alpha\beta h + \tfrac{1}{6}\beta^2 h(2h-1)\right\}\right]$ |
| (A,N,A) | $\sigma^2\left[1 + \alpha^2(h-1) + \gamma k(2\alpha+\gamma)\right]$ |
| (A,A,A) | $\sigma^2\left[1 + (h-1)\left\{\alpha^2 + \alpha\beta h + \tfrac{1}{6}\beta^2h(2h-1)\right\} + \gamma k\left\{2\alpha + \gamma + \beta m(k+1)\right\}\right]$ |

- Exact formulae exist for the additive models; $k$ is again $\lfloor (h-1)/m \rfloor$.
- For the rest, the intervals come from **simulating** thousands of future paths from the fitted
  states and taking percentiles.
- Either way, they assume the innovation residuals really are white noise with constant variance.
  That is what you check first — the intervals are only as good as that assumption.

## AICc chose. Was it right?
AICc ranks models on how well they explain the **data they were fitted to**. The challenge scores
you on data the model has not seen. Week 3 gave us the tool to check whether those two agree.

## AICc chose. Was it right?
- Every ETS specification beats both benchmarks — the models are earning their keep.
- But the **AICc winner is the worst of the three ETS models out of sample**, and the specification
  AICc ranked third (A,A,A) forecasts best.
- The differences are small (RMSE 0.54 vs 0.57 million trips), which is the point: AICc narrowed 18
  models down to three, and cross-validation picked among them.

## The working rule

Use AICc to **shortlist** specifications on one series; use cross-validation on the horizon you will
actually be scored on to **choose**. Reporting only one of the two is how people talk themselves into
the wrong model.

# ETS in practice

## Five ways to get this wrong
| Trap | What you see | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| `season_length` ≠ the real period | A plausible model with a worse AICc, silently | Quarterly 4, monthly 12, daily-with-weekly 7 — check against Lab 3 |
| Seasonal period too long | `season_length=52` returns `ETS(A,N,N)`: the season is dropped | ETS needs $m \le 24$; long or multiple seasonality → Week 8 |
| Multiplicative model, non-positive data | `ValueError: Inappropriate model for data with negative or zero values` | Use the additive models, or treat the zeros first |
| `damped=None` | `AAN` comes back as `ETS(A,Ad,N)` | Pass `damped=False` to mean it |
| Transform **and** multiply | The seasonal swing is modelled twice | Box-Cox + additive, **or** raw + multiplicative |

The first two are the dangerous ones: nothing fails, and the model you get is quietly worse.

## Two of them, silently
## What ETS is, in one slide
- A **level, a slope and a season that update** as each observation arrives, with the update speeds
  $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ estimated from the data.
- A **taxonomy**: (Error, Trend, Season), each additive or multiplicative, trend optionally damped —
  18 models, one estimation routine, one search (`AutoETS`).
- **Fast, robust and hard to beat** on short seasonal business series. It is the benchmark ARIMA has
  to justify itself against next.
- What it does **not** do: covariates, multiple seasonality, long seasonal periods, structural
  breaks. Those are the next four lectures.
