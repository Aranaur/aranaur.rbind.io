# MATH840 Mid-term — Study Guide

## What students know about the quiz

- Proctored, electronic (Moodle), in the Week 5 practice slot, closed book.
- **12 points**, written answers in English.
- Covers Weeks 1–4 (FPP3 chapters 1–3, 5, 8). Any topic below can appear.
- Expect a mix of skills: explaining and comparing concepts, interpreting figures, reading model output and residual diagnostics, choosing a forecasting method and justifying it.

This guide does not contain the quiz. It maps what the course taught so the coach can drill every topic with equal weight.

## Marking principles (any written answer)

1. **Answer first** — a clear verdict (yes/no, agree/disagree, suitable/not suitable, which one).
2. **Justify through the mechanism** — why, in terms of how the method or statistic works.
3. **Use the evidence** — when a figure or output is given, name the specific feature you rely on.
4. **Correct terminology and notation.**
5. **Nuance** — if it depends, say on what.

Marks are lost for generic textbook paragraphs that ignore the figure, for verdicts without reasons, for describing a pattern without explaining its cause, and for mixing up in-sample and out-of-sample quantities.

## Topic map

Each topic lists the core points a strong answer draws on. Rotate across all of them; none is more likely than another.

### Week 1 — Forecasting and time series graphics

1. **What makes something forecastable:** understanding of the drivers, amount of data, similarity of future to past, whether the forecast affects the outcome. Rank examples by forecastability and justify.
2. **The forecasting process:** goal, what to forecast, horizon, data, model, deploy, monitor. How forecasting differs from ordinary regression (no future predictors, ordering matters, uncertainty grows with the horizon).
3. **Time series patterns:** trend, seasonality (fixed period), cycles (irregular, usually longer). Distinguishing seasonal from cyclic behaviour.
4. **Graphics and what each reveals:** time plot, seasonal plot, seasonal subseries plot, lag plot, scatterplot matrix. Choosing the right plot for a question.
5. **The seasonal period $m$:** quarterly 4, monthly 12, weekly 52, daily with a weekly cycle 7. Data can have several seasonal periods.
6. **Autocorrelation:** $r_k$; how trend (slowly decaying positive ACF) and seasonality (peaks at seasonal lags) show up; reading an ACF plot.
7. **White noise:** definition; $r_k \approx N(0, 1/T)$, bounds $\pm 1.96/\sqrt{T}$; about 1 in 20 spikes outside the bounds by chance.
8. **Benchmarks and the skill score:** why an RMSE means nothing without a benchmark; skill score = RMSE(model)/RMSE(benchmark), below 1 beats the benchmark.

### Week 2 — Transformations and decomposition

9. **Adjustments:** calendar, population (per capita), inflation. When each is needed.
10. **Mathematical transformations:** log and Box-Cox; variance stabilisation; interpreting log changes as percentages; choosing $\lambda$ (Guerrero/CV, regression approach); keep it simple.
11. **Additive vs multiplicative decomposition:** when seasonal variation grows with the level; a log turns multiplicative into additive.
12. **Moving averages:** $m$-MA, centred MA ($2\times m$-MA for even $m$), the end-point problem.
13. **Classical decomposition:** steps; weaknesses (no trend at the ends, a seasonal pattern fixed forever, not robust to outliers).
14. **STL:** loess; inner and outer (robustness) loops; the seasonal window (how fast seasonality may change) and trend window; handles any period and several seasonal components; additive only.
15. **Seasonal adjustment:** what the seasonally adjusted series contains (trend + remainder) and when to use it.
16. **Strength of trend and seasonality:** $F_T$, $F_S$; what values near 0 or 1 mean.
17. **Benchmarks as claims about components:** mean, naïve, drift, seasonal naïve — which component each assumes dominates.

### Week 3 — The forecaster's toolbox

18. **Simple methods:** mean, naïve, seasonal naïve, drift — formulas, when each is sensible.
19. **Fitted values and residuals:** $\hat{y}_{t|t-1}$, $e_t = y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1}$; why fitted values are not true forecasts.
20. **Residual assumptions:** uncorrelated and mean zero (needed for good point forecasts); constant variance and normality (useful for intervals). What violations imply.
21. **Residual diagnostics:** time plot, ACF and histogram of residuals — what each can reveal.
22. **Portmanteau tests:** Box-Pierce and Ljung-Box; $H_0$; $l = 10$ non-seasonal, $l = 2m$ seasonal; $\chi^2_l$; what a small p-value means; the role of diagnostics in the modelling workflow.
23. **Forecasting with transformations:** back-transformation; back-transformed point forecasts are medians; bias adjustment gives means; intervals keep their coverage.
24. **Forecasting with decomposition:** forecast the seasonally adjusted series with a non-seasonal method and the seasonal component with seasonal naïve, then recombine.
25. **Training and test sets:** why accuracy must be measured on unseen data; residuals vs forecast errors.
26. **Point accuracy measures:** scale-dependent (MAE, RMSE — what each is minimised by), percentage (MAPE — the zero and meaningful-zero problems, asymmetry), scaled (MASE, RMSSE). Choosing a measure for a purpose.
27. **Distributional accuracy:** quantile (pinball) score, Winkler score for intervals, CRPS; skill scores.
28. **Time series cross-validation:** rolling forecasting origin; choosing $h$, windows and step; why it beats a single split.

### Week 4 — Exponential smoothing and ETS

29. **SES:** weighted average with geometrically decaying weights; component form; flat forecasts; the role of $\alpha$ and $\ell_0$; estimated by minimising SSE.
30. **Holt's linear trend:** level and slope equations, $\beta^*$; why a straight line extrapolated far ahead is dangerous.
31. **Damped trend:** $\phi$; forecasts converge to $\ell_T + \phi b_T/(1-\phi)$; the usual range 0.8–0.98.
32. **Holt-Winters additive vs multiplicative:** seasonal component as an amount vs a ratio; when each fits; $m$ must be the true period.
33. **The smoothing-parameter restrictions:** $\beta = \alpha\beta^*$, $\gamma = (1-\alpha)\gamma^*$, so $0<\gamma<1-\alpha$; usual vs admissible region.
34. **Interpreting $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \phi$:** each is the speed at which its component updates (near 0 — almost fixed; larger — reacts to recent data).
35. **The taxonomy:** 9 methods (trend N/A/A$_d$ × season N/A/M); 18 ETS models with additive or multiplicative errors; notation ETS(E,T,S).
36. **Methods vs models:** a model adds an error distribution, giving a likelihood, prediction intervals and simulation. Same point forecasts under additive and multiplicative errors, different intervals.
37. **State space form:** measurement and state equations; writing a model's equations from its ETS label and estimates (see table below).
38. **Models to avoid and data limits:** unstable ETS(A,·,M) combinations; multiplicative components need strictly positive data; ETS has one seasonal component with a short period.
39. **Estimation and selection:** maximum likelihood; AIC, AICc, BIC — $k$ includes initial states; AICc comparable only on the same data and scale.
40. **Innovation vs regular residuals:** which to use for diagnostics.
41. **ETS forecasts and prediction intervals:** set future errors to zero; intervals widen with $h$; analytical for additive models, simulated otherwise.
42. **ETS in practice:** the traps (wrong `season_length`, too-long seasonal period, non-positive data with multiplicative models, `damped=None`, transforming *and* using multiplicative seasonality); AICc to shortlist, cross-validation to choose; what ETS cannot do (covariates, multiple or long seasonality, structural breaks).

## Reference: ETS equations in state space form

$\varepsilon_t \sim \text{NID}(0, \sigma^2)$. Replace $b_{t-1}$ by $\phi b_{t-1}$ everywhere for damped versions.

| Model | Equations |
|---|---|
| ETS(A,N,N) | $y_t = \ell_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$; $\ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + \alpha\varepsilon_t$ |
| ETS(M,N,N) | $y_t = \ell_{t-1}(1+\varepsilon_t)$; $\ell_t = \ell_{t-1}(1+\alpha\varepsilon_t)$ |
| ETS(A,A,N) | $y_t = \ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}+\varepsilon_t$; $\ell_t = \ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}+\alpha\varepsilon_t$; $b_t = b_{t-1}+\beta\varepsilon_t$ |
| ETS(M,A,N) | $y_t = (\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})(1+\varepsilon_t)$; $\ell_t = (\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})(1+\alpha\varepsilon_t)$; $b_t = b_{t-1}+\beta(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})\varepsilon_t$ |
| ETS(A,N,A) | $y_t = \ell_{t-1}+s_{t-m}+\varepsilon_t$; $\ell_t = \ell_{t-1}+\alpha\varepsilon_t$; $s_t = s_{t-m}+\gamma\varepsilon_t$ |
| ETS(A,A,A) | $y_t = \ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}+s_{t-m}+\varepsilon_t$; $\ell_t = \ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}+\alpha\varepsilon_t$; $b_t = b_{t-1}+\beta\varepsilon_t$; $s_t = s_{t-m}+\gamma\varepsilon_t$ |
| ETS(M,N,A) | $y_t = (\ell_{t-1}+s_{t-m})(1+\varepsilon_t)$; $\ell_t = \ell_{t-1}+\alpha(\ell_{t-1}+s_{t-m})\varepsilon_t$; $s_t = s_{t-m}+\gamma(\ell_{t-1}+s_{t-m})\varepsilon_t$ |
| ETS(M,N,M) | $y_t = \ell_{t-1}s_{t-m}(1+\varepsilon_t)$; $\ell_t = \ell_{t-1}(1+\alpha\varepsilon_t)$; $s_t = s_{t-m}(1+\gamma\varepsilon_t)$ |
| ETS(M,A,M) | $y_t = (\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})s_{t-m}(1+\varepsilon_t)$; $\ell_t = (\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})(1+\alpha\varepsilon_t)$; $b_t = b_{t-1}+\beta(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})\varepsilon_t$; $s_t = s_{t-m}(1+\gamma\varepsilon_t)$ |

## Common misconceptions, one or two per week

- Week 1: "Seasonal and cyclic mean the same thing." "Any ACF spike outside the bounds proves the series is not white noise."
- Week 2: "A log transform removes the trend." "Classical decomposition is fine for data with outliers." "STL can handle multiplicative seasonality directly."
- Week 3: "Residuals and forecast errors are the same thing." "Back-transformed forecasts are means." "Accuracy measured on the training data tells you how well the model forecasts." "One accuracy measure is best for every problem."
- Week 4: "A smoother fitted line means a better forecast." "Additive and multiplicative errors give different point forecasts." "Any seasonal period works in ETS." "Log-transform the data and then use multiplicative seasonality."
