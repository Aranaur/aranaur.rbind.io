Where this fits

Three weeks, and the gap they leave

Week What you got

What is still missing. Decomposition describes the past — it has no mechanism for carrying the components forward. The benchmarks forecast, but they each use exactly one component and throw the rest away.

Exponential smoothing keeps the components and updates them as data arrives.

The one idea

Week 1 gave you two extremes:

Average method — every observation matters equally

\[ \hat{y}_{T+h|T} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} y_t \]

Naïve method — only the last observation matters

\[ \hat{y}_{T+h|T} = y_T \]

Neither is usually right. Recent observations should matter more — but not exclusively. How much more is not a matter of taste: it is a parameter you estimate from the data.

What we build this week

  1. Simple exponential smoothing — a level that updates. No trend, no season.
  2. Holt’s method — add a slope. Damped trend — and don’t let the slope run forever.
  3. Holt-Winters — add a season, additive or multiplicative.
  4. A taxonomy — nine methods in one table, named by (Trend, Season).
  5. ETS models — the same nine, wrapped in a probability model: 18 models, with intervals and a likelihood you can compare.
  6. Automatic selection — AutoETS, AICc, and why that is not the same as forecast accuracy.

Simple exponential smoothing

Simple exponential smoothing

Forecast equation

\[ \hat{y}_{T+1|T} = \alpha y_T + \alpha(1-\alpha) y_{T-1} + \alpha(1-\alpha)^2 y_{T-2} + \cdots, \qquad 0 \le \alpha \le 1 \]

  • The weights \(\alpha(1-\alpha)^j\) decay geometrically with the age of the observation.
  • They sum to 1 (in the limit), so the forecast is a genuine weighted average.
  • \(\alpha \to 1\): only the last observation counts — the naïve method.
  • \(\alpha \to 0\): every observation counts almost equally — the average method.

The two Week 1 extremes are the two ends of one dial.

Component form

The weighted average is awkward to compute and impossible to extend. Rewrite it with a state:

Component form

  • Forecast equation: \(\hat{y}_{t+h|t} = \ell_t\)
  • Smoothing equation: \(\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1}\)
  • \(\ell_t\) is the level: our current estimate of where the series is.
  • One number carries the whole history. Each new observation nudges it.
  • Forecasts are flat: \(\hat{y}_{T+h|T} = \ell_T\) for every \(h\). No trend, no season — by construction.

Weighted form — iterate the smoothing equation

\[ \hat{y}_{T+h|T} = \sum_{j=0}^{T-1} \alpha(1-\alpha)^j y_{T-j} + (1-\alpha)^T \ell_0 \]

The initial level \(\ell_0\) never disappears entirely — its weight is \((1-\alpha)^T\).

Choosing α and ℓ₀

Both are estimated, exactly the way regression coefficients are: by minimising the sum of squared one-step errors.

\[ \text{SSE} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \left(y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1}\right)^2 = \sum_{t=1}^{T} e_t^2 \]

  • Unlike regression, there is no closed-form solution — \(\ell_t\) depends on \(\alpha\) recursively.
  • So it is numerical optimisation: pick \((\alpha, \ell_0)\), run the recursion, measure SSE, repeat.
  • This is why “which \(\alpha\) should I use?” is the wrong question. The data answers it.

What α does: read it

  • \(\alpha = 0.1\) — a smooth line that lags the series by years. It smooths away real movements.
  • \(\alpha = 0.9\) — the level chases every wiggle; the fitted value is almost the previous observation.
  • \(\hat{\alpha}\) — the compromise the SSE prefers.

A smooth line is not a good forecast

A low \(\alpha\) looks nicer on the plot and fits worse. Judge the level by its one-step errors, not by how calm it looks.

SES forecasts: read them

  • The point forecast is a horizontal line — the current level, carried forward. That is all the model claims to know.

Methods with trend

Holt’s linear trend method

Component form

  • Forecast: \(\hat{y}_{t+h|t} = \ell_t + h b_t\)
  • Level: \(\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})\)
  • Trend: \(b_t = \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta^*) b_{t-1}\)
  • Two smoothing parameters, \(\alpha\) and \(\beta^*\), both in \([0, 1]\); four things to estimate with \(\ell_0\) and \(b_0\).
  • The level is a weighted average of \(y_t\) and the one-step forecast \(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}\).
  • The slope is a weighted average of the latest change in level and the previous slope.
  • \(\beta^*\) small: a stable, slowly turning slope. \(\beta^*\) large: a slope that reacts to the last two observations.

The problem with a straight line

Holt’s forecast is \(\ell_T + h b_T\): the current slope, extended forever.

  • Over \(h = 4\) quarters that is often reasonable.
  • Over \(h = 40\) it says a company growing 5% a quarter today will grow 5% a quarter in ten years.
  • Empirically, trends flatten. Unconstrained linear trends are one of the classic ways to produce a confidently absurd forecast.

Fix: let the slope decay.

Damped trend method

Component form

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_{t+h|t} &= \ell_t + (\phi + \phi^2 + \cdots + \phi^h) b_t \\ \ell_t &= \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + \phi b_{t-1}) \\ b_t &= \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta^*)\phi b_{t-1} \end{aligned} \]

  • Damping parameter \(0 < \phi < 1\). With \(\phi = 1\) this is Holt’s method.
  • Short horizons stay trended; long horizons flatten out to \(\ell_T + \dfrac{\phi b_T}{1-\phi}\).
  • In practice \(\phi\) is restricted to \((0.8, 0.98)\): below 0.8 the damping is so aggressive the trend barely survives one step, and at 1 the numerics get unstable.

Holt versus damped: read it

  • AICc prefers the damped model here — and the damped forecasts are higher, not lower, than Holt’s. Damping constrains the slope’s decay, it does not force pessimism: with \(\phi\) fixed the optimiser is free to re-fit \(\alpha\) and \(\beta^*\), and it does.
  • \(\hat{\phi} = 0.98\), the top of the allowed range: this series shows no evidence of a flattening trend over 58 years, so the model damps as little as it is allowed to.
  • The implied long-run level is worth computing — it is what the model believes about 2200.

Two traps in statsforecast

damped=None (the default) lets AutoETS choose damping by AICc — so AAN with no damped argument may silently return ETS(A,Ad,N). And the phi= argument is a starting value, not a constraint: pass phi=0.9 and the fit still comes back with \(\hat\phi = 0.97\). Fix \(\phi\) in statsmodels, compare specifications in statsforecast.

Methods with seasonality

Holt-Winters’ additive method

Component form

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_{t+h|t} &= \ell_t + h b_t + s_{t+h-m(k+1)} \\ \ell_t &= \alpha(y_t - s_{t-m}) + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}) \\ b_t &= \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta^*) b_{t-1} \\ s_t &= \gamma(y_t - \ell_{t-1} - b_{t-1}) + (1-\gamma) s_{t-m} \end{aligned} \]

  • \(m\) is the seasonal period; \(k\) is the integer part of \((h-1)/m\), which just means use the estimate for this season from the most recent complete cycle.
  • Each observation now updates three states: level, slope and the season it belongs to.
  • A season is refreshed once per cycle, so \(\gamma\) acts on \(m\)-step-old information.
  • Restrictions: \(0 < \alpha, \beta^* < 1\) and \(0 < \gamma < 1 - \alpha\).

Where the γ restriction comes from

The seasonal component is often written against the level rather than the pre-level:

\[ s_t = \gamma^*(y_t - \ell_t) + (1-\gamma^*)s_{t-m} \]

Substituting \(\ell_t = \alpha(y_t - s_{t-m}) + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})\):

\[ s_t = \gamma^*(1-\alpha)(y_t - \ell_{t-1} - b_{t-1}) + \left[1 - \gamma^*(1-\alpha)\right]s_{t-m} \]

So the two forms agree with \(\gamma = \gamma^*(1-\alpha)\), and the natural restriction \(0 \le \gamma^* \le 1\) becomes \(0 \le \gamma \le 1-\alpha\).

The level and the season compete for the same information. That is why their weights are linked.

Holt-Winters’ multiplicative method

Component form

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_{t+h|t} &= (\ell_t + h b_t)\, s_{t+h-m(k+1)} \\ \ell_t &= \alpha \frac{y_t}{s_{t-m}} + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}) \\ b_t &= \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta^*) b_{t-1} \\ s_t &= \gamma \frac{y_t}{\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}} + (1-\gamma) s_{t-m} \end{aligned} \]

  • Additive: the season is an amount. Within a cycle \(\sum s_i \approx 0\).
  • Multiplicative: the season is a percentage. Within a cycle \(\sum s_i \approx m\).
  • This is the same question you answered in Lab 3 with Box-Cox: does the seasonal swing grow with the level? If it does, multiply.

Note

Multiplicative seasonality is an alternative to transforming the data, not an addition to it. Box-Cox then additive seasonality does the same job — and if you do both, you are modelling the swing twice.

The states: read them

  • The level is the trend of the series with the season removed — the seasonally adjusted series from Lab 3, computed recursively instead of by STL.
  • The slope is tiny and slow: Australian holiday travel shifts over decades, not quarters.
  • The season is the interesting one. It is flat until the mid-2000s and then changes shape: the additive fit changes amplitude, the multiplicative fit changes it proportionally.
  • The residual is what neither could explain. Additive residuals are in trips, multiplicative ones are relative — that is why the two columns are not on the same scale.

Holt-Winters damped

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{y}_{t+h|t} &= \left[\ell_t + (\phi + \phi^2 + \cdots + \phi^h) b_t\right] s_{t+h-m(k+1)} \\ \ell_t &= \alpha (y_t / s_{t-m}) + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + \phi b_{t-1}) \\ b_t &= \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta^*)\phi b_{t-1} \\ s_t &= \gamma \frac{y_t}{\ell_{t-1} + \phi b_{t-1}} + (1-\gamma)s_{t-m} \end{aligned} \]

This combination — multiplicative season, damped additive trend — is the workhorse specification for business data with a strong weekly or monthly rhythm.

Daily data: what to notice

  • \(m = 7\), not 365: the cycle we are modelling is the week. Weekdays are high, weekends low.
  • Two weeks of forecasts reproduce the weekly shape; the level drifts slightly and then damps.
  • The intervals are wide on weekdays and narrow on weekends — with multiplicative errors, uncertainty scales with the level.

Electricity has more than one season

Daily demand also has an annual cycle, and half-hourly demand has a daily one on top of the weekly. ETS carries one seasonal component. Multiple seasonality needs the methods in Week 8.

A taxonomy of methods

Nine methods, two questions

Trend component Seasonal: N (none) Seasonal: A (additive) Seasonal: M (multiplicative)
N (none) (N,N) (N,A) (N,M)
A (additive) (A,N) (A,A) (A,M)
A\(_d\) (additive damped) (A\(_d\),N) (A\(_d\),A) (A\(_d\),M)

Two questions, asked twice: is there a trend, and does it flatten? and is there a season, and does it scale with the level?

Everything so far is one cell of this table. Nothing new is coming — only combinations.

Innovations state space models

Methods and models

A method

An algorithm that returns point forecasts. Everything so far is a method.

A model

A method plus a statement about the errors. It gives the same point forecasts, and in addition:

  • prediction intervals that follow from something, rather than from a rule of thumb;
  • a likelihood, so models can be compared with AICc;
  • a way to simulate futures, not just draw a line.

Week 3 gave you residual diagnostics and told you the intervals assume something about the errors. Here is where that assumption is written down.

ETS(A,N,N): SES with additive errors

Start from the component form and look at the one-step error \(e_t = y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1} = y_t - \ell_{t-1}\):

Error-correction form

\[ \begin{aligned} y_t &= \ell_{t-1} + e_t \\ \ell_t &= \ell_{t-1} + \alpha(y_t - \ell_{t-1}) = \ell_{t-1} + \alpha e_t \end{aligned} \]

Now assume \(e_t = \varepsilon_t \sim \text{NID}(0, \sigma^2)\) and the method becomes a model:

  • Measurement equation: \(y_t = \ell_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\)
  • State equation: \(\ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + \alpha \varepsilon_t\)

The level is not smoothed data any more — it is a state that the noise moves.

ETS(M,N,N): the same method, relative errors

Suppose instead the error is proportional to the level — \(\varepsilon_t = \dfrac{y_t - \hat{y}_{t|t-1}}{\hat{y}_{t|t-1}} \sim \text{NID}(0, \sigma^2)\):

  • Measurement equation: \(y_t = \ell_{t-1}(1 + \varepsilon_t)\)
  • State equation: \(\ell_t = \ell_{t-1}(1 + \alpha\varepsilon_t)\)

Important

With the same parameters, additive and multiplicative errors give identical point forecasts and different prediction intervals. The choice of error type is a choice about uncertainty, not about the forecast.

Holt’s method as a model

ETS(A,A,N) — additive errors, \(\varepsilon_t = y_t - \ell_{t-1} - b_{t-1}\)

\[ \begin{aligned} y_t &= \ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t \\ \ell_t &= \ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1} + \alpha\varepsilon_t \\ b_t &= b_{t-1} + \beta\varepsilon_t \end{aligned} \]

ETS(M,A,N) — multiplicative errors, \(\varepsilon_t = \dfrac{y_t - (\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})}{\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}}\)

\[ \begin{aligned} y_t &= (\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})(1 + \varepsilon_t) \\ \ell_t &= (\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})(1 + \alpha\varepsilon_t) \\ b_t &= b_{t-1} + \beta(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})\varepsilon_t \end{aligned} \]

In the models we write \(\beta = \alpha\beta^*\) — the two parametrisations describe the same method.

The general form

Let \(\mathbf{x}_t = (\ell_t, b_t, s_t, s_{t-1}, \ldots, s_{t-m+1})\) be the state vector and \(\varepsilon_t \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(0, \sigma^2)\):

\[ y_t = \underbrace{h(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})}_{\mu_t} + \underbrace{k(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\varepsilon_t}_{e_t} \qquad\qquad \mathbf{x}_t = f(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}) + g(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\varepsilon_t \]

  • Additive errors: \(k(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = 1\), so \(y_t = \mu_t + \varepsilon_t\).
  • Multiplicative errors: \(k(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mu_t\), so \(y_t = \mu_t(1 + \varepsilon_t)\).

All 18 models are this pair of equations with different \(h\), \(k\), \(f\), \(g\). One estimation routine covers every one of them — which is exactly why AutoETS can exist.

Models that misbehave

  • Some combinations divide by a state: ETS(A,N,M), ETS(A,A,M), ETS(A,A\(_d\),M). They are numerically unstable and normally excluded.
  • Multiplicative errors need strictly positive data. With a zero in the series, the relative error is undefined and the fit fails outright:

Estimation and model selection

Estimating an ETS model

Estimate the smoothing parameters \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \phi\) and the initial states \(\ell_0, b_0, s_0, s_{-1}, \ldots, s_{-m+1}\) by maximising the likelihood:

\[ L^*(\theta, \mathbf{x}_0) = T \log\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\varepsilon_t^2\right) + 2\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log\left|k(\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\right| = -2\log(\text{Likelihood}) + \text{constant} \]

  • For additive errors, minimising \(L^*\) is equivalent to minimising SSE — the same objective as the methods.
  • For multiplicative errors it is not: the second term penalises states that make the error scale large.
  • A monthly seasonal model estimates 3 parameters and 14 initial states. On five years of data that is a lot of freedom — which is what AICc is about to charge us for.

Parameter restrictions

Usual (traditional) region

  • In the methods: \(0 < \alpha, \beta^*, \gamma^*, \phi < 1\), so every equation is a weighted average.
  • In the models \(\beta = \alpha\beta^*\) and \(\gamma = (1-\alpha)\gamma^*\), giving \(0 < \alpha < 1\), \(0 < \beta < \alpha\), \(0 < \gamma < 1-\alpha\).
  • \(0.8 < \phi < 0.98\) to keep the optimiser away from the boundary.

Admissible region

Wider: any parameters for which the distant past stops influencing current forecasts. For ETS(A,N,N) the traditional region is \(0 < \alpha < 1\), the admissible one \(0 < \alpha < 2\).

An \(\hat{\alpha}\) pinned at 0.9999, as in the Australian population fit, is a signal: the series wants a random walk, and the level is doing nothing but copying the last observation.

Model selection

\[ \text{AIC} = -2\log(L) + 2k \qquad \text{AIC}_c = \text{AIC} + \frac{2k(k+1)}{T-k-1} \qquad \text{BIC} = \text{AIC} + k\left[\log(T) - 2\right] \]

where \(k\) counts the parameters and the initial states.

  • AICc is the default for ETS: with \(m + 3\) things to estimate and short series, the small-sample correction matters.
  • Likelihoods are comparable only on the same data: never compare an AICc from a transformed series with one from the raw series, or across different \(m\).
  • Lower is better, and a difference of less than about 2 is not a difference.

Forecasting with ETS

Point forecasts

Iterate the state equations for \(t = T+1, \ldots, T+h\) and set every future \(\varepsilon_t = 0\).

ETS(A,A,N)

\[ \begin{aligned} y_{T+1} &= \ell_T + b_T + \varepsilon_{T+1} &\Rightarrow&& \hat{y}_{T+1|T} &= \ell_T + b_T \\ y_{T+2} &= (\ell_T + b_T + \alpha\varepsilon_{T+1}) + (b_T + \beta\varepsilon_{T+1}) + \varepsilon_{T+2} &\Rightarrow&& \hat{y}_{T+2|T} &= \ell_T + 2b_T \end{aligned} \]

ETS(M,A,N)

\[ y_{T+1} = (\ell_T + b_T)(1+\varepsilon_{T+1}) \Rightarrow \hat{y}_{T+1|T} = \ell_T + b_T, \qquad \hat{y}_{T+2|T} = \ell_T + 2b_T \]

Same point forecasts, as promised. The error type never touches the mean.

Prediction intervals

\[ \hat{y}_{T+h|T} \pm c\,\sigma_h \]

Model Forecast variance \(\sigma_h^2\)
(A,N,N) \(\sigma^2\left[1 + \alpha^2(h-1)\right]\)
(A,A,N) \(\sigma^2\left[1 + (h-1)\left\{\alpha^2 + \alpha\beta h + \tfrac{1}{6}\beta^2 h(2h-1)\right\}\right]\)
(A,N,A) \(\sigma^2\left[1 + \alpha^2(h-1) + \gamma k(2\alpha+\gamma)\right]\)
(A,A,A) \(\sigma^2\left[1 + (h-1)\left\{\alpha^2 + \alpha\beta h + \tfrac{1}{6}\beta^2h(2h-1)\right\} + \gamma k\left\{2\alpha + \gamma + \beta m(k+1)\right\}\right]\)
  • Exact formulae exist for the additive models; \(k\) is again \(\lfloor (h-1)/m \rfloor\).
  • For the rest, the intervals come from simulating thousands of future paths from the fitted states and taking percentiles.
  • Either way, they assume the innovation residuals really are white noise with constant variance. That is what you check first — the intervals are only as good as that assumption.

The working rule

Use AICc to shortlist specifications on one series; use cross-validation on the horizon you will actually be scored on to choose. Reporting only one of the two is how people talk themselves into the wrong model.

What ETS is, in one slide

  • A level, a slope and a season that update as each observation arrives, with the update speeds \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) estimated from the data.
  • A taxonomy: (Error, Trend, Season), each additive or multiplicative, trend optionally damped — 18 models, one estimation routine, one search (AutoETS).
  • Fast, robust and hard to beat on short seasonal business series. It is the benchmark ARIMA has to justify itself against next.
  • What it does not do: covariates, multiple seasonality, long seasonal periods, structural breaks. Those are the next four lectures.
