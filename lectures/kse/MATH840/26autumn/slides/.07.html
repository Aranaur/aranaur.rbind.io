Seasonal ARIMA

The notation

\[ \text{ARIMA}\;\underbrace{(p,\,d,\,q)}_{\text{non-seasonal part}}\;\underbrace{(P,\,D,\,Q)_m}_{\text{seasonal part}} \]

  • \(m\) — the seasonal period: 12 for monthly data, 4 for quarterly.
  • \(P\), \(D\), \(Q\) — the same three ideas as \(p\), \(d\), \(q\), but at lags that are multiples of \(m\): a seasonal AR term looks at \(y_{t-12}\), a seasonal MA term at \(\varepsilon_{t-12}\), a seasonal difference is \(y_t - y_{t-12}\).
  • Part 1’s model with a seasonal difference was ARIMA\((2,0,1)(0,1,0)_{12}\). Today’s fix adds \(Q = 1\).

In backshift notation

ARIMA\((1,1,1)(1,1,1)_4\), without a constant:

\[ \underbrace{(1 - \phi_1 B)}_{\substack{\text{non-seasonal} \\ \text{AR(1)}}} \underbrace{(1 - \Phi_1 B^4)}_{\substack{\text{seasonal} \\ \text{AR(1)}}} \underbrace{(1 - B)}_{\substack{\text{non-seasonal} \\ \text{difference}}} \underbrace{(1 - B^4)}_{\substack{\text{seasonal} \\ \text{difference}}} y_t = \underbrace{(1 + \theta_1 B)}_{\substack{\text{non-seasonal} \\ \text{MA(1)}}} \underbrace{(1 + \Theta_1 B^4)}_{\substack{\text{seasonal} \\ \text{MA(1)}}} \varepsilon_t \]

The seasonal and non-seasonal parts multiply. That is why a seasonal model has terms at lags nobody asked for.

Multiplied out

\[ \begin{aligned} y_t &= (1 + \phi_1)y_{t-1} - \phi_1y_{t-2} + (1 + \Phi_1)y_{t-4} \\ &\quad - (1 + \phi_1 + \Phi_1 + \phi_1\Phi_1)y_{t-5} + (\phi_1 + \phi_1\Phi_1)y_{t-6} \\ &\quad - \Phi_1y_{t-8} + (\Phi_1 + \phi_1\Phi_1)y_{t-9} - \phi_1\Phi_1y_{t-10} \\ &\quad + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1} + \Theta_1\varepsilon_{t-4} + \theta_1\Theta_1\varepsilon_{t-5} \end{aligned} \]

  • Four parameters, \(\phi_1, \Phi_1, \theta_1, \Theta_1\), and they drive ten lags of \(y\) and four of \(\varepsilon\).
  • The cross terms — \(\phi_1\Phi_1\), \(\theta_1\Theta_1\) — are what “multiplicative seasonal ARIMA” means. They are not free: the model asserts that the seasonal and non-seasonal dynamics combine as a product.

Seasonal signatures

The same reading rules as part 1, applied at lags \(m, 2m, 3m, \dots\):

ACF at seasonal lags PACF at seasonal lags
Seasonal MA(\(Q\)), e.g. \((0,0,0)(0,0,1)_{12}\) spike at 12, nothing at 24, 36 decays: 12, 24, 36, …
Seasonal AR(\(P\)), e.g. \((0,0,0)(1,0,0)_{12}\) decays: 12, 24, 36, … spike at 12, nothing at 24

def seasonal_sample(sar=0.0, sma=0.0, m=12, n=3000, seed=4):
    ar, ma = np.zeros(m + 1), np.zeros(m + 1)
    ar[0] = ma[0] = 1
    ar[m], ma[m] = -sar, sma
    return ArmaProcess(ar, ma).generate_sample(n, distrvs=np.random.default_rng(seed).standard_normal)

How AutoARIMA searches seasonal models

The same Hyndman–Khandakar algorithm as part 1, with the seasonal orders added:

  1. \(D\) from seasonal strength (nsdiffs, \(F_S > 0.64\)), then \(d\) from KPSS on the seasonally differenced series (ndiffs). Never \(D > 1\); \(d + D \le 2\).
  2. Start from ARIMA\((2,d,2)(1,D,1)_m\) and three simple models.
  3. Move one of \(p, q, P, Q\) by \(\pm 1\), or toggle the constant; keep any improvement in AICc.
Option Default What it does
stepwise True False: try every model up to the limits — seconds on a monthly series
approximation False True: approximate the likelihood during the search, for long series
max_P, max_Q 2 caps on the seasonal orders — part 1 set them to 0

ARIMA and ETS

Two families, partly the same

  • ETS describes components — level, trend, season — and how they update. ARIMA describes the autocorrelation of the (differenced) series.
  • The six fully additive ETS models are ARIMA models: same forecasts, same intervals, different parameterisation.
  • The multiplicative ETS models have no ARIMA counterpart, and most ARIMA models have no ETS counterpart. Every ETS model is non-stationary; ARIMA covers stationary series too.

Where they overlap, the choice between them is a choice of parameterisation, not of model.

Where they coincide

ETS model ARIMA model Parameters
ETS(A,N,N) ARIMA(0,1,1) \(\theta_1 = \alpha - 1\)
ETS(A,A,N) ARIMA(0,2,2) \(\theta_1 = \alpha + \beta - 2,\quad \theta_2 = 1 - \alpha\)
ETS(A,A\(_d\),N) ARIMA(1,1,2) \(\phi_1 = \phi,\ \theta_1 = \alpha + \phi\beta - 1 - \phi,\ \theta_2 = (1 - \alpha)\phi\)
ETS(A,N,A) ARIMA\((0,1,m)(0,1,0)_m\)
ETS(A,A,A) ARIMA\((0,1,m+1)(0,1,0)_m\)
ETS(A,A\(_d\),A) ARIMA\((1,0,m+1)(0,1,0)_m\)

How to choose between them

Not on AICc

An ARIMA likelihood is computed on the differenced series; an ETS likelihood on the original one. The two AICc are not comparable, for the same reason AICc across different \(d\) is not (part 1).

On a rolling origin, with a benchmark in the table

Fit both, score both on the horizon you will be judged on, over several windows. Across the Track B pool the automatic models land at a median skill of 0.81 (ARIMA) and 0.84 (ETS) — a difference smaller than the spread between series. Neither family is better in general. One of them is better on your series, and only the holdout can say which.

Dynamic regression

What Week 5 left open

\[ y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1,t} + \dots + \beta_k x_{k,t} + \eta_t \]

Week 5 assumed \(\eta_t\) was white noise, and the residuals said otherwise. Two things go wrong:

  • the standard errors are too small, so every \(t\) statistic is inflated;
  • the forecasts ignore the strongest predictor available — the error of the last few periods.

Dynamic regression lets the error follow an ARIMA model:

\[ \eta_t \sim \text{ARIMA}(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_m, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \text{white noise} \]

Two error terms now: \(\eta_t\), the regression error, and \(\varepsilon_t\), the ARIMA error. Only \(\varepsilon_t\) should look like white noise.

The fortnights: what to see

  • Both regressions follow the shape of each fortnight — the working-day steps and the temperature swings — because they are given the actual temperature. Seasonal naive only copies last week, and is worst wherever the weather changed.
  • OLS and the dynamic regression differ mostly in level: OLS starts from the average relationship, the dynamic regression from where the last few days’ errors left it. In most fortnights that puts teal closer — that is the error model at work.
  • The last fortnight is where all three fail: demand drops after Christmas further than temperature and a working-day flag can explain. A predictor the model does not have is a gap no error model fills.

def walmart_X(k, trend=False):
    """Fourier terms of order k for a 52-week season, the pre-Christmas dummy, optionally a trend."""
    fourier = [f(2 * np.pi * i * t / 52) for i in range(1, k + 1) for f in (np.sin, np.cos)]
    return np.column_stack(([t] if trend else []) + fourier + [prechristmas])

Walmart: what the table says

  • With more harmonics the OLS residuals do become autocorrelated — but negatively, and strongly. That is not persistence the next week could exploit; it is what a curve with dozens of terms leaves when it bends to fit two or three values per week.
  • The error model reacts the way it should to negative lag-1 autocorrelation: it differences (\(d = 1\)) and adds an MA term. The residuals pass Ljung-Box.
  • And the forecasts get no better: worse at \(K = 3\) and \(K = 10\), a tie at \(K = 18\). Differencing the errors throws away the level the Fourier terms had estimated, and a 143-week series has little to replace it with.

Whiter residuals are not better forecasts. The error model earns its place when the errors persist — as with electricity — not when they are an artefact of over-fitting.

Walmart forecasts: what to see

  • Christmas 2011. The training data hold a single Christmas. Thanksgiving falls on a different week each year, so the smooth Fourier season puts its spike a week early and invents a dip after it; the Christmas peak itself is undershot by both regressions. Seasonal naive copies last year’s weeks and is closest. A dummy estimated from one event is one data point.
  • The last 13 weeks. Nothing happens in late summer, and all three forecasts sit close to the actuals. The difference is the interval: the dynamic regression’s is the widest — differenced errors make it less sure of the level, and the rolling-origin table shows that uncertainty was warranted.

On Walmart the problem is not the errors. It is a seasonal pattern seen two and a half times, with a holiday that moves.

Five ways to get this wrong

Trap What you see Fix
A seasonal series fitted with season_length=1 Residual ACF spikes at \(m\), \(2m\); loses to snaive season_length=m, and let \(D\) and \(P, Q\) in
Two seasonal differences D=2 forced by hand; wild forecasts \(D \le 1\), always; \(d + D \le 2\)
Ljung-Box with model_df = p + q only Residuals look whiter than they are model_df = p + q + P + Q
Choosing between ARIMA and ETS on AICc A comparison that means nothing Rolling origin, same horizon, benchmark in the table
Regression with ARIMA errors, future \(x\) unknown Ex-post accuracy reported as a forecast Say ex-post, or forecast \(x\), or use a scenario

What seasonal ARIMA is, in one slide

  • Is: part 1’s model, with the same three ideas — difference, AR, MA — applied a second time at the seasonal lags. Read \(P\) and \(Q\) off the ACF and PACF at \(m, 2m, 3m\) the way you read \(p\) and \(q\) at \(1, 2, 3\).
  • Is: equal to ETS for the additive models, and neither better nor worse than ETS in general — on the Track B pool each family wins on part of the series.
  • Is: the error model of choice for regression, which turns Week 5’s regression into something whose standard errors and forecasts you can use.
  • Is not: a cure for a short history. Every seasonal parameter is estimated from one value per year.
