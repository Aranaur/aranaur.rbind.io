Every one of them describes the shape of the series and treats what is left over as noise. Week 5 ended on the leftover that was not noise: residuals whose ACF said this week’s error looks like last week’s.

Stationarity

What stationary means

Definition

\(y_t\) is stationary if for every \(s\) the distribution of \((y_t, \dots, y_{t+s})\) does not depend on \(t\).

In words: pick any window of the series, slide it along, and the statistical picture does not change. In practice that means

  • no trend — the mean does not drift;
  • constant variance — the swings are the same size early and late;
  • nothing predictable in the long run — no seasonality, because a season tells you where the series will be at a given time.

Important

Cycles are allowed. A cycle of irregular length — a business cycle, a commodity boom — does not tell you when the next peak comes, so a series with cycles can be stationary. Seasonality can’t be, because its period is fixed.

Differencing

First and second differences

Differencing removes a changing level. The new series is the change from one observation to the next:

\[ y'_t = y_t - y_{t-1} \]

It has \(T-1\) observations. If the changes still trend, difference again:

\[ y''_t = y'_t - y'_{t-1} = (y_t - y_{t-1}) - (y_{t-1} - y_{t-2}) = y_t - 2y_{t-1} + y_{t-2} \]

That leaves \(T-2\) observations, and it models the change in the change. In practice you almost never need to go beyond second order.

Seasonal differences

The change from the same season last year:

\[ y'_t = y_t - y_{t-m}, \qquad m = \text{the seasonal period} \]

  • With \(m = 12\) this is “this January minus last January”. It takes out a fixed seasonal pattern.
  • If the seasonal difference still wanders, take a first difference of it as well:

\[ y''_t = (y_t - y_{t-m}) - (y_{t-1} - y_{t-m-1}) = y_t - y_{t-1} - y_{t-m} + y_{t-m-1} \]

Note

A difference needs to mean something. A first difference is the change since last period, and a seasonal difference is the change since last year. Differences at other lags, say lag 3 on annual data, usually have no sensible interpretation.

Which difference, and how many

  • Seasonal first when the season is strong. The seasonal difference may already be stationary, and here it is: ndiffs returned 0 after the seasonal difference. Order does not matter mathematically, since \((1-B)(1-B^{12}) = (1-B^{12})(1-B)\), but it decides whether you stop after one. The ACF of the seasonal difference still decays slowly, but KPSS does not reject, so the remaining persistence is left to the AR terms rather than to a second difference.
  • Do not over-difference. Differencing a series that is already white noise does not leave white noise:
twice = change.diff().dropna()
print(f"Google changes:          r1 = {change.autocorr(1):+.2f}")
print(f"changes, differenced:    r1 = {twice.autocorr(1):+.2f}   (theory: -0.50)")
Google changes:          r1 = +0.10
changes, differenced:    r1 = -0.41   (theory: -0.50)

If \(y_t = \varepsilon_t\), then \(y'_t = \varepsilon_t - \varepsilon_{t-1}\), and that series has \(\rho_1 = -0.5\). A large negative spike at lag 1 after differencing is the sign you went one step too far.

Unit root tests

Tests that decide whether to difference. They come in two kinds, with opposite null hypotheses:

Test \(H_0\) Small \(p\) means
ADF (augmented Dickey–Fuller) non-stationary stationary, do not difference
KPSS stationary non-stationary, difference 
pd.DataFrame([
    {"series": name,
     "ADF p": adfuller(s)[1],
     "KPSS p": kpss(s, nlags="auto")[1]}
    for name, s in [("price", google.y), ("change", change)]
]).round(3)
series ADF p KPSS p
0 price 0.903 0.01
1 change 0.000 0.10

Both tests agree on Google. statsmodels clips the KPSS \(p\) to \([0.01, 0.10]\), the range of its lookup table.

AutoARIMA uses KPSS. It differences only when the data reject stationarity, so by default it leans towards not differencing.

The tests in one call

y_ret = retail.y.to_numpy(float)
print("ndiffs, Google price:              ", ndiffs(google.y.to_numpy(float)))
print("nsdiffs, retail employment (m=12):  ", nsdiffs(y_ret, period=12))
print("ndiffs after the seasonal difference:", ndiffs(y_ret[12:] - y_ret[:-12]))
ndiffs, Google price:               1
nsdiffs, retail employment (m=12):   1
ndiffs after the seasonal difference: 0
  • ndiffs applies KPSS again and again: difference, test, repeat, up to \(d = 2\).
  • nsdiffs uses the seasonal strength from Week 2:

\[ F_S = \max\left(0,\ 1 - \frac{\text{Var}(R_t)}{\text{Var}(S_t + R_t)}\right) \]

and takes one seasonal difference if \(F_S > 0.64\). You computed \(F_S\) in Lab 3, and every Track B series was screened on it (the issue rule is \(F_S \ge 0.6\)), so expect \(D = 1\) on your own series.

Backshift notation

The operator \(B\)

\[ B y_t = y_{t-1}, \qquad B^2 y_t = y_{t-2}, \qquad B^{12} y_t = y_{t-12} \]

Differences become polynomials in \(B\), and polynomials multiply:

Operation Written as
First difference \(y_t - y_{t-1}\) \((1-B)\,y_t\)
Second-order difference \(y_t - 2y_{t-1} + y_{t-2}\) \((1-B)^2\,y_t\)
\(d\)-th order difference \((1-B)^d\,y_t\)
Seasonal difference \(y_t - y_{t-m}\) \((1-B^m)\,y_t\)
Seasonal then first \((1-B)(1-B^m)\,y_t = y_t - y_{t-1} - y_{t-m} + y_{t-m-1}\)

Warning

\((1-B)^2\) is a second-order difference. \(1-B^2\) is a lag-2 difference, \(y_t - y_{t-2}\). They are different things.

Autoregressive models

AR(\(p\))

\[ y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \dots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \text{white noise} \]

A regression of \(y_t\) on its own lags. It is the Week 5 model with the lags of \(y\) as the only predictors.

Code 
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 3.4), sharey=True)

axes[0].set_title(r"AR(1): $y_t = 18 - 0.8\,y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$")
axes[1].plot(20 + simulate(ar=[1.3, -0.7], n=100, seed=7), color=turquoise)
axes[1].set_title(r"AR(2): $y_t = 8 + 1.3\,y_{t-1} - 0.7\,y_{t-2} + \varepsilon_t$")

    ax.set_xlabel("t")
plt.show()

The left series flips sign every step. The right one moves in slow waves. Two coefficients are enough to make a cycle.

AR(1): what \(\phi_1\) does

\[ y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t \]

\(\phi_1\) \(c\) The process is
0 0 white noise
1 0 a random walk: naive forecasts
1 \(\neq 0\) a random walk with drift: the drift method
\(\lvert\phi_1\rvert < 1\) any stationary, pulled back to its mean
\(< 0\) any oscillating around the mean

For \(|\phi_1| < 1\) the mean is not \(c\):

\[ \mu = c + \phi_1\mu \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mu = \frac{c}{1-\phi_1} \]

Two of the four Week 1 benchmarks are AR(1) models with \(\phi_1 = 1\).

Moving average models

MA(\(q\))

\[ y_t = c + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1} + \theta_2\varepsilon_{t-2} + \dots + \theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q} \]

A regression on past forecast errors. You never observe them; the model reconstructs them as it goes, so this is not literally a regression you can run with OLS.

Important

Not the moving average of Week 2. That one smoothed the series to estimate a trend. This one generates the series from past shocks.

Code 
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 3.0), sharey=True)
axes[0].plot(20 + simulate(ma=[0.8], n=100, seed=3), color=slate)
axes[0].set_title(r"MA(1): $y_t = 20 + \varepsilon_t + 0.8\,\varepsilon_{t-1}$")
axes[1].plot(simulate(ma=[-1, 0.8], n=100, seed=3), color=turquoise)
axes[1].set_title(r"MA(2): $y_t = \varepsilon_t - \varepsilon_{t-1} + 0.8\,\varepsilon_{t-2}$")
plt.show()

An AR(1) is an MA(\(\infty\))

Substitute the model into itself:

\[ \begin{aligned} y_t &= \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t = \phi_1(\phi_1 y_{t-2} + \varepsilon_{t-1}) + \varepsilon_t \\ &= \phi_1^2 y_{t-2} + \phi_1\varepsilon_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t = \dots = \varepsilon_t + \phi_1\varepsilon_{t-1} + \phi_1^2\varepsilon_{t-2} + \phi_1^3\varepsilon_{t-3} + \cdots \end{aligned} \]

That works only if \(|\phi_1| < 1\), so that \(\phi_1^k \to 0\). The same algebra works for any stationary AR(\(p\)):

print("MA weights of AR(1), phi = 0.8:   ", np.round(arma2ma([1, -0.8], [1], lags=6), 3))
print("0.8 ** k:                         ", np.round(0.8 ** np.arange(6), 3))
MA weights of AR(1), phi = 0.8:    [1.    0.8   0.64  0.512 0.41  0.328]
0.8 ** k:                          [1.    0.8   0.64  0.512 0.41  0.328]

Hold on to these \(\psi\) weights. The prediction intervals at the end of the lecture are built from them.

Invertibility

The reverse is also possible: an MA(\(q\)) can be written as an AR(\(\infty\)), but only if \(|\theta_1| < 1\) (for \(q = 1\)). Why insist on it? Because without it, the model is not identified:

for theta in (0.5, 2.0):
    acf_theory = ArmaProcess([1], [1, theta]).acf(4)[1:]
    print(f"MA(1) with theta = {theta}:  ACF = {np.round(acf_theory, 3)}")
MA(1) with theta = 0.5:  ACF = [0.4 0.  0. ]
MA(1) with theta = 2.0:  ACF = [0.4 0.  0. ]

\(\rho_1 = \theta/(1+\theta^2)\) takes the same value at \(\theta\) and at \(1/\theta\). The data cannot tell the two apart, so we keep the one where recent errors matter more than old ones, \(|\theta| < 1\).

  • MA(2): \(-1 < \theta_2 < 1,\ \theta_1 + \theta_2 > -1,\ \theta_1 - \theta_2 < 1\).
  • Beyond that, again, the software enforces it.

ARIMA

ARMA, then ARIMA

Put lagged values and lagged errors together:

\[ y'_t = c + \phi_1 y'_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_p y'_{t-p} + \theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q} + \varepsilon_t \]

where \(y'_t\) is the series after \(d\) differences. This is ARIMA(\(p, d, q\)):

  • AR — \(p\), the number of lagged values;
  • I — “integrated”, \(d\), the number of first differences;
  • MA — \(q\), the number of lagged errors.

In backshift notation, ARIMA(1,1,1):

\[ \underbrace{(1 - \phi_1 B)}_{\text{AR(1)}}\ \underbrace{(1 - B)}_{\text{first difference}}\ y_t \;=\; c + \underbrace{(1 + \theta_1 B)}_{\text{MA(1)}}\ \varepsilon_t \]

Models you already know

ARIMA Is
(0, 0, 0), with \(c\) white noise around a mean: the mean method
(0, 1, 0), no \(c\) random walk: the naive method
(0, 1, 0), with \(c\) random walk with drift: the drift method
(\(p\), 0, 0) AR(\(p\))
(0, 0, \(q\)) MA(\(q\))
(0, 1, 1), no \(c\) simple exponential smoothing, with \(\theta_1 = \alpha - 1\)

Three of the four Week 1 benchmarks, and the simplest ETS model, are ARIMA models.

The last row is a claim worth checking.

The two are estimated by different software and from different starting points, so the numbers are not identical to the last digit. The forecasts agree to two decimals.

What ARIMA adds is that it does not stop at (0,1,1). ETS has a fixed menu of components; ARIMA lets the data pick any \(p\) and \(q\).

Reading the fitted model

statsforecast reports the mean \(\mu\) as intercept, not the constant \(c\). To write the equation, convert:

c = coef["intercept"] * (1 - coef["ar1"] - coef["ar2"])
cycle = 2 * np.pi / np.arccos(-coef["ar1"] * (1 - coef["ar2"]) / (4 * coef["ar2"]))
print(f"c = mu (1 - phi1 - phi2) = {c:.2f}")
print(f"phi1^2 + 4 phi2 = {coef['ar1'] ** 2 + 4 * coef['ar2']:+.2f}  ->  average cycle {cycle:.1f} years")
c = mu (1 - phi1 - phi2) = 2.56
phi1^2 + 4 phi2 = -0.40  ->  average cycle 18.2 years

\[ y_t = 2.56 + 1.68\,y_{t-1} - 0.80\,y_{t-2} - 0.69\,\varepsilon_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \hat\sigma = \sqrt{8.05} = 2.84 \]

  • \(d = 0\): the model treats exports as stationary, pulled back to a mean of 20.2% of GDP.
  • \((\phi_1, \phi_2)\) is in the pink region of the triangle, so the series cycles, with an average length of about 18 years. Nothing in the data is labelled as a cycle. Two AR coefficients are enough to produce one.

What \(c\) and \(d\) do to the long run

\(c = 0\) \(c \neq 0\)
\(d = 0\) forecasts go to zero go to the mean of the data
\(d = 1\) go to a constant follow a straight line
\(d = 2\) follow a straight line a quadratic trend (never used)
  • The higher \(d\), the faster the prediction intervals widen. With \(d = 0\) they stop growing.
  • Choosing \(d\) and \(c\) amounts to choosing what the forecast does far ahead. That decision is yours, and you should make it on purpose. \(p\) and \(q\) only shape the first few steps.

Choosing p and q by eye

Partial autocorrelation

The ACF at lag \(k\) mixes direct and indirect links: \(y_t\) is related to \(y_{t-2}\) partly through \(y_{t-1}\). The partial autocorrelation removes the intermediaries.

Definition

\(\alpha_k\) = the coefficient \(\phi_k\) in the regression \(y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_k y_{t-k} + \varepsilon_t\).

That is literally how you can compute it:

lags = pd.DataFrame({f"lag{j}": egypt.y.shift(j) for j in range(1, 6)})
by_regression = [sm.OLS(egypt.y, sm.add_constant(lags.iloc[:, :k]), missing="drop").fit().params.iloc[-1]

Estimation and order selection

Estimation and information criteria

The parameters \(c, \phi_1, \dots, \phi_p, \theta_1, \dots, \theta_q\) are found by maximum likelihood. For Gaussian errors that is close to minimising \(\sum e_t^2\), but the errors depend on the parameters recursively, so it is numerical optimisation, as for ETS. Different software gives slightly different estimates.

The orders are chosen with the criteria from Week 4, where \(k = 1\) if \(c \neq 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{AIC} &= -2\log L + 2(p+q+k+1) \\ \text{AIC}_c &= \text{AIC} + \frac{2(p+q+k+1)(p+q+k+2)}{T-p-q-k-2} \\ \text{BIC} &= \text{AIC} + [\log T - 2](p+q+k+1) \end{aligned} \]

Two comparisons AICc cannot make

Not across different \(d\)

\(L\) is the likelihood of the differenced series. ARIMA(2,1,0) and ARIMA(2,0,0) are fitted to different data, so their AICc are not comparable. Choose \(d\) first, using the tests, and then compare \(p\) and \(q\) for that \(d\).

Not across model families

An ARIMA AICc and an ETS AICc are computed on different terms, so you cannot compare them either. To put ARIMA against ETS, use a holdout or cross-validation. That is part of next week’s lecture.

How AutoARIMA searches

The Hyndman–Khandakar algorithm (JSS, 2008):

  1. Choose \(d\) with repeated KPSS tests (ndiffs). Choose \(D\) with seasonal strength (nsdiffs).
  2. Start from the best AICc among ARIMA(2,\(d\),2), (0,\(d\),0), (1,\(d\),0), (0,\(d\),1).
  3. Move: try \(p \pm 1\), \(q \pm 1\), both together, and with or without \(c\). If a neighbour has a lower AICc, it becomes the current model.
  4. Repeat until no neighbour improves.

It is a stepwise local search. It visits a few dozen models instead of every one, so it can stop at a model that is not the best. stepwise=False searches the full grid, which is slower.

The modelling procedure

By hand, or automatically

By hand — what the challenge asks for first

  1. Plot the data; transform if the variance grows.
  2. Difference until stationary: nsdiffs, then ndiffs.
  3. Read the ACF and PACF of the differenced series.
  4. Fit the candidates; shortlist on AICc.
  5. Residuals white noise? If not, back to step 3.
  6. Compare on a holdout, then forecast.

Automatically — the check

  1. Plot the data; transform if needed.
  2. AutoARIMA chooses \(d\), \(p\), \(q\) and \(c\).
  3. The same residual check.
  4. The same holdout.

Each manual step leaves evidence you can cite in the justification. If AutoARIMA lands somewhere else, the interesting question is why.

Forecasting

Point forecasts

  1. Write the model with \(y_t\) alone on the left.
  2. Replace \(t\) by \(T + h\).
  3. On the right: replace future \(y\) by their forecasts, future errors by zero, and past errors by the residuals \(e_t\).

For Egypt’s ARIMA(2,0,1):

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat y_{T+1|T} &= c + \phi_1 y_T + \phi_2 y_{T-1} + \theta_1 e_T \\ \hat y_{T+2|T} &= c + \phi_1 \hat y_{T+1|T} + \phi_2 y_T \qquad (\theta_1 e_{T+1} \text{ is set to } 0) \end{aligned} \]

After \(q\) steps the MA part has no information left, and the forecast is driven by the AR part alone.

By hand, against predict

y_egy = egypt.y.to_numpy(float)
e = fit_egy.model_["residuals"]
f1 = c + coef["ar1"] * y_egy[-1] + coef["ar2"] * y_egy[-2] + coef["ma1"] * e[-1]
f2 = c + coef["ar1"] * f1 + coef["ar2"] * y_egy[-1]

fc_egy = fit_egy.predict(h=10, level=[95])
print(f"h=1  by hand {f1:.4f}   predict {fc_egy['mean'][0]:.4f}")
print(f"h=2  by hand {f2:.4f}   predict {fc_egy['mean'][1]:.4f}")
h=1  by hand 18.0075   predict 18.0075
h=2  by hand 20.0419   predict 20.0419

There is no black box. Once you have the coefficients and the last residual, the forecast is the recursion written out step by step.

Prediction intervals

Write the model as an MA(\(\infty\)), \(y_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \psi_1\varepsilon_{t-1} + \psi_2\varepsilon_{t-2} + \cdots\) (the \(\psi\) weights from earlier). The \(h\)-step error is the sum of the \(h\) shocks that have not happened yet:

\[ v_{T+h|T} = \hat\sigma^2\left[1 + \psi_1^2 + \dots + \psi_{h-1}^2\right], \qquad \hat y_{T+h|T} \pm 1.96\sqrt{v_{T+h|T}} \]

psi = arma2ma([1, -coef["ar1"], -coef["ar2"]], [1, coef["ma1"]], lags=5)
half_width = 1.96 * np.sqrt(fit_egy.model_["sigma2"] * np.cumsum(psi ** 2))
print("from psi weights:", np.round(half_width, 3))
print("from predict:    ", np.round(np.asarray(fc_egy["hi-95"] - fc_egy["mean"])[:5], 3))
from psi weights: [ 5.56   7.811  9.132  9.788 10.011]
from predict:     [ 5.559  7.811  9.131  9.787 10.011]
  • \(h = 1\): always \(\hat\sigma^2\), for every ARIMA.
  • MA(\(q\)): \(\psi_j = \theta_j\) for \(j \le q\) and 0 beyond, so the intervals stop growing after \(q\) steps.

  • \(d = 0\): the \(\psi\) weights die out, and the interval settles at the spread of the data itself. Far enough ahead, all the model knows is the unconditional distribution.
  • \(d \ge 1\): the \(\psi\) weights do not die out, and the interval widens without limit.

Why the intervals are too narrow

The formula assumes the residuals are uncorrelated and normal, and it leaves three things out:

  • parameter uncertainty: \(\hat\phi\) and \(\hat\theta\) are treated as known;
  • model uncertainty: the CAR table showed four orders AICc could not separate, and the interval uses only one of them;
  • the future resembling the past: the same structure is assumed to hold over the whole horizon.

In practice, 95% ARIMA intervals often cover less than 95% out of sample. Check the coverage on your holdout before you defend the intervals.

  • The non-seasonal model loses to seasonal naive. The seasonal difference plus an ARMA beats it clearly.

  • The fix is a seasonal MA term, a \(\Theta_1\varepsilon_{t-12}\). That is ARIMA\((p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_m\), next week.

ARIMA in practice

Five ways to get this wrong

Trap What you see Fix
Comparing AICc across different \(d\) A “better” model that is fitted to different data Fix \(d\) with the tests first, then compare \(p\) and \(q\)
Over-differencing A large negative spike at lag 1 (or \(m\)) in the differenced ACF Go back one difference
Reading intercept as \(c\) The equation does not reproduce the forecasts statsforecast reports the mean, so \(c = \mu(1 - \sum\phi_i)\)
Ljung-Box with model_df=0 Residuals look whiter than they are model_df = p + q
season_length=1 on seasonal data Residual ACF spike at lag \(m\), and the model loses to snaive Seasonal difference, then the seasonal terms from part 2

What ARIMA is, in one slide

  • Is: a model of the series’ autocorrelation. Differencing removes what does not stay put, and AR and MA terms describe what is left.
  • Is: a family that contains naive, drift, mean and SES as special cases, so the Week 1 benchmarks are ARIMA models that were never estimated.
  • Is: something you can specify by hand from evidence (tests, ACF, PACF) and check automatically. When the two disagree you learn something either way.
  • Is not: interpretable in terms of trend and season the way ETS is. An ARIMA(2,0,1) does not have a “level” you can plot.
  • Is not: finished on seasonal data yet. That is part 2.
