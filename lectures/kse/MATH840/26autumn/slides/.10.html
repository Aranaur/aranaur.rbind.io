Understanding foundation models

Algorithm, model, foundation model

An algorithm is the recipe; the data are the ingredients; the model is the cake. Least squares is an algorithm; your regression line is a model. The same recipe on different data gives a different model.

A foundation model (Stanford HAI, 2021) is a model trained on large, diverse data so that it can be applied to many tasks. Peixeiro’s four marks of one:

What it means for forecasting
large, diverse training data billions of observations, every frequency and domain
a large model tens of millions to hundreds of millions of weights
many tasks any series, any frequency, any horizon — without retraining
adaptable can be fine-tuned: a few more training steps on your data

Almost all of them are transformers — so before any of them, what a transformer does with a series.

Tokens — single values or patches; embedding — each token as a long vector; encoder — how tokens relate; decoder — the forecast, piece by piece, each fed back in. Many models keep only one half: that choice is most of the difference between them.

Positional encoding: telling the model when

Attention by itself sees a set of tokens, not a sequence: shuffle them and it cannot tell. So each position \(p\) gets a fixed vector added to its embedding:

\[ PE_{(p,\,2i)} = \sin\!\left(\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d}}\right), \qquad PE_{(p,\,2i+1)} = \cos\!\left(\frac{p}{10000^{2i/d}}\right) \]

Code 
positions, d = 64, 64
p = np.arange(positions)[:, None]
i = np.arange(d // 2)[None, :]
pe = np.zeros((positions, d))
pe[:, 0::2] = np.sin(p / 10000 ** (2 * i / d))
pe[:, 1::2] = np.cos(p / 10000 ** (2 * i / d))
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 2.9), width_ratios=[1.3, 1])

Peixeiro’s image: clock hands. Each dimension turns at its own speed; read together they give the exact position — as hour, minute and second hands give the exact time.

Self-attention: which past matters

Each token asks a query \(q\), every token offers a key \(k\) and a value \(v\); the output is a weighted average of values, weighted by how well query and key match:

\[ \text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right) V \]

Code 
co2 = pd.read_csv("../data/co2_mauna_loa.csv", parse_dates=["ds"])
recent = co2[co2["ds"] >= "2015-01-01"].reset_index(drop=True)
values = recent["y"].to_numpy()
tokens = np.array([values[t - 11:t + 1] - values[t - 11:t + 1].mean() for t in range(11, len(values))])
tokens = tokens / tokens.std(axis=1, keepdims=True)
query = tokens[-1]
scores = tokens[:-1] @ query / np.sqrt(tokens.shape[1])
weights = np.exp(scores - scores.max())
weights /= weights.sum()
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(12, 2.8))
lag = np.arange(len(weights), 0, -1)
ax.bar(lag, weights, color=[red_pink if l % 12 == 0 else "lightgray" for l in lag], width=0.8)
ax.set_xlabel("how many months back the token is")
ax.set_ylabel("attention weight")
ax.invert_xaxis()
ax.set_title("The latest 12-month window of CO₂ attends to its own months in earlier years (lags 12, 24, 36, … in pink)")
plt.show()

No weights were trained here: each token is a 12-month stretch of CO₂, and \(q \cdot k\) is their similarity. Even so, attention finds the season by itself. A trained model learns projections for \(Q, K, V\), and runs many such heads in parallel — one may track the trend, another the season.

Building a tiny foundation model

Pretrain, transfer, fine-tune — at home

Peixeiro’s chapter 2 builds a foundation model in miniature, to make three words concrete:

  • Pretraining: train once on many series.
  • Transfer learning: apply the trained model to a series it has never seen. With no further training, that is zero-shot forecasting.
  • Fine-tuning: a few more training steps on the new series, starting from the pretrained weights.

Our pretraining corpus: all 1428 M3 Monthly series from Week 9. The model: N-BEATS, 12 months ahead from 24 months of input.

from neuralforecast import NeuralForecast
from neuralforecast.models import NBEATS

quiet = dict(enable_progress_bar=False, logger=False, enable_model_summary=False)
m3 = pd.read_csv("../data/m3_monthly.csv", parse_dates=["ds"])
pretrained = NeuralForecast(
    models=[NBEATS(h=12, input_size=24, max_steps=1000, scaler_type="robust", random_seed=1, **quiet)],
    freq="MS")
pretrained.fit(m3)                      # about a minute on a laptop CPU

scaler_type="robust" is our one change to the book’s code: without scaling, the network fitted on M3’s scales is thrown by CO₂’s ~400 ppm, and a single fine-tuning step moved its forecast by 100 ppm.

Zero-shot, fine-tuned, trained from scratch

The new series: CO₂ at Mauna Loa (Week 8’s practice), monthly since 2005 — not in M3. Last 12 months held out.

Another frequency

The book’s second test: the model pretrained on monthly data, asked about daily data. Ours: Melbourne’s daily maximum temperature, 2012–2014 (vic_elec, Week 7), 12 days ahead, over ten windows — one 12-day window of weather is mostly noise.

In the book, the monthly model failed on daily data. Ours did not: over ten windows it beat both the naive forecast and a network trained on the daily series itself. Twelve days of weather are mostly noise around a level, and a robustly scaled N-BEATS forecasts a level well whatever frequency it learned on. One experiment is not a law — the book’s or ours. That is the point of scoring every claim.

What a real foundation model needs

What our miniature could not do, and every model that follows was built to fix:

  • One horizon. It forecasts exactly 12 steps — the h it was trained with. A real one must take any horizon: 7 days or 168 hours. Solved by generating the output step by step (TimeGPT, Chronos) or in long output patches (TimesFM).
  • One frequency, one domain. Trained on M3’s monthly series only. Real corpora mix every frequency from seconds to years and domains from electricity to web traffic — billions of observations.
  • Data. Text is everywhere; public time series are scarce. That is why the teams build their own corpora, mix series together and generate synthetic ones — and why they rarely say exactly what is in them.
  • Compute. Our pretraining took a minute on a laptop. Chronos-T5 (Large) took 18 hours on eight A100 GPUs.

TimeGPT

A generative pretrained transformer for time series

TimeGPT (Garza et al., 2023; Nixtla) was the first foundation model for forecasting — and the only one here you cannot download.

  • A full encoder–decoder transformer. Generative: the decoder produces the forecast one step at a time, feeding each back in, so the horizon can be any length.
  • It estimates the conditional distribution of the future given the past and covariates: \[P\left(y_{t+1:t+h} \mid y_{0:t},\, x_{0:t+h}\right) = f_\theta\left(y_{0:t},\, x_{0:t+h}\right)\] The covariates \(x\) are needed up to \(t + h\): their future must be known, or forecast first.
  • Trained on about 100 billion observations from finance, health, energy, web and more — the sources are not disclosed. Kept raw: noise and outliers included.

Intervals by conformal prediction

TimeGPT’s intervals do not come from a distribution in the model. They come from its errors on the past — Week 8’s conformal intervals:

Code 
actual = np.array([[8, 4, 5], [7, 5, 6]])
predicted = np.array([[6, 7, 4], [8.5, 2.5, 5.2]])
scores = np.abs(actual - predicted).ravel()                 # conformity scores, all windows
forecast = np.array([6.0, 7.0, 8.0])
candidates = forecast[:, None] + np.concatenate([scores, -scores])[None, :]
lo, hi = np.percentile(candidates, [10, 90], axis=1)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(12, 2.7))
for step in range(3):
    ax.scatter(np.full(candidates.shape[1], step + 1), candidates[step], color="lightgray", s=40, zorder=2)
ax.fill_between([1, 2, 3], lo, hi, color=red_pink, alpha=0.2, label="80% interval: 10th–90th percentile")
ax.plot([1, 2, 3], forecast, "o-", color=red_pink, label="point forecast")
ax.set_xticks([1, 2, 3], ["h = 1", "h = 2", "h = 3"])
ax.legend(fontsize=8, loc="upper left")
ax.set_title(f"The book's example: errors {np.round(scores, 1).tolist()} from two past windows")
plt.show()
  1. Forecast past windows, where the answer is known (cross-validation); keep the absolute errors.
  2. Add and subtract every error to the new point forecast: a cloud of possible values.
  3. The 80% interval is its 10th to 90th percentile — here 3.55 to 8.45 around the first forecast of 6.

Lag-Llama

Open, probabilistic, decoder-only

Lag-Llama (Rasul et al., 2024) borrows Meta’s LLaMA language model — decoder only — and changes what a token is:

  • A token for time \(t\) holds lagged values \(y_{t-1}, y_{t-7}, y_{t-12}, \dots\) from a fixed context, plus calendar features at every scale: second-of-minute up to quarter-of-year. Which of them change from step to step tells the model the frequency.
  • The output is not a number but the three parameters of a Student-\(t\) distribution — mean, scale, degrees of freedom — so every forecast comes with its spread (Week 9’s DeepAR, pretrained).
  • Pretrained on 20 datasets — energy, transport, nature, air quality — at daily, hourly, 15-minute and 1-minute frequencies only, on horizons of 24 to 60 steps.

That last line is a warning: it has never seen weekly or monthly data. Chapter 2’s experiment predicts what follows. Peixeiro recommends fine-tuning it before use outside its frequencies.

In practice it is a research repository, not a package: you clone it and work inside it. The book’s current code no longer uses it; neither do we.

Chronos

A language model that reads a time series

Chronos (Ansari et al., 2024; Amazon) changes nothing in the language model. It changes the series, so that an off-the-shelf T5 can read it:

Code 
store1 = walmart[walmart["unique_id"] == 1]["y"].to_numpy()
scaled = store1 / np.mean(np.abs(store1))
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(13, 2.8))
axes[0].plot(store1 / 1e6, color=slate)
axes[0].set_title("Store 1, $ million")
for ax, bins, colour in zip(axes[1:], [100, 5], [blue, red_pink]):
    edges = np.linspace(scaled.min(), scaled.max(), bins + 1)
    token = np.clip(np.digitize(scaled, edges) - 1, 0, bins - 1)
    ax.step(range(len(token)), token, where="mid", color=colour)
    ax.set_title(f"Mean-scaled, then {bins} bins: the tokens", fontsize=10)
plt.show()
  1. Mean scaling: divide by the mean absolute value — millions of dollars and dozens of cars on one scale.
  2. Quantisation: clip to \([-15, 15]\) and cut into 4094 equal bins. Each value becomes a bin number: a word from a vocabulary. With 5 bins (right) the series says almost nothing.
  3. Train the T5 to predict the next token with cross-entropy — regression done as classification. Forecasting is sampling tokens; many samples give the quantiles.

Where the data came from

Fifty steps on the weeks before the first window move the score in the second decimal. The learning rate is small on purpose: fine-tuning should adjust a pretrained model, not overwrite it. Like every other choice, it is a candidate to be scored, not an improvement to be assumed.

Moirai

Built for any number of variables

Moirai (Woo et al., 2024; Salesforce) — an encoder-only transformer with three ideas:

Code

plt.show()
  • Patches whose length depends on the frequency: 8 for yearly data, 16–32 for weekly, 32–128 for per-minute. One embedding layer per patch size.
  • Any-variate attention: the target and every covariate are flattened into one long sequence; a learned bias tells attention whether two patches come from the same variable or from different ones. Add a covariate and the sequence just gets longer.
  • A mixture of Student-\(t\), log-normal, negative binomial and a low-variance normal as output.

LOTSA, and what came after

Moirai’s team published its corpus: LOTSA, 27 billion observations from nine domains — the largest open collection at the time.

Frequency Datasets Observations
yearly, quarterly 9 3.2 million
monthly, weekly 17 29.5 million

hourly 31 19.9 billion

72% of it is hourly; yearly series are 0.003%. Training therefore caps each dataset’s share, so the model does not become an hourly specialist.

Moirai-MoE (2024) switches to a decoder with a mixture of experts — several feed-forward networks, a gate picking two per token. Moirai 2.0 (2025) is the current version.

TimesFM

A point forecaster with long output patches

TimesFM (Das et al., 2024; Google) is decoder-only and, unlike the others, trained for a point forecast — mean squared error, not a distribution.

tfm = timesfm.TimesFM_2p5_200M_torch.from_pretrained("google/timesfm-2.5-200m-pytorch")

                                   use_continuous_quantile_head=True, return_backcast=True))

for name in ["TimesFM", "TimesFM + holiday"]:
    start, frames = time.time(), []
    for cutoff, history, window in windows():
        inputs = [c.numpy() for c in contexts(history)]
        if name == "TimesFM":
            point, _ = tfm.forecast(horizon=H, inputs=inputs)
            point = [p[-H:] for p in np.asarray(point)]
        else:          # flag over context + horizon; a linear "xreg" step: pip install "timesfm[xreg]"
            flags = [np.concatenate([h["holiday"], w["holiday"]]).tolist() for (_, h), (_, w)
                     in zip(history.groupby("unique_id"), window.groupby("unique_id"))]
            point, _ = tfm.forecast_with_covariates(inputs=[i.tolist() for i in inputs],
                                                    dynamic_numerical_covariates={"holiday": flags})
            point = [np.asarray(p)[-H:] for p in point]
        frames.append(window.assign(**{name: np.concatenate(point)}))
    record(name, frames, start)

Time-LLM, and what language models add

Time-LLM (Jin et al., 2024; chapter 9) keeps a frozen LLM and learns a translation into it:

  • Patch reprogramming: each patch of the series is mapped onto a small set of learned text prototypes — combinations of the LLM’s word embeddings that come to mean “short up, then steady down”.
  • Prompt-as-prefix: a text description of the data and the task is put in front of the patches.
  • It has no zero-shot mode: the translation layer must be trained on your data every time. It is in neuralforecast as TimeLLM.

Peixeiro’s verdict across chapters 8–10: asking an LLM in words works, roughly, and loses to the models built for time series; Time-LLM does better than plain prompting, at the cost of training. Our one window above says the same: a language model answers a question about numbers with words — here, fewer than were asked for.

Did it see the answer?

Leakage, tested

A zero-shot score is only zero-shot if the test data were not in the pretraining corpus. M4 Monthly is in the corpora of Chronos and Chronos-2, with each series’ last 18 months held out; M3 is in neither. If a model remembers, it should do unusually well on the M4 months it saw.

memory effect 2.5% 97.5%
Chronos-Bolt 1.008 0.948 1.065
Chronos-2 0.995 0.943 1.051
snaive 1.136 1.061 1.220
  • 1000 series from each set, two windows each, MASE relative to ETS. Below 1 would mean “better on what it saw”. Both Chronos models sit at 1: no memory of the windows they were trained on.
  • The seasonal-naive row is a placebo: it moves (1.14) because the windows differ — so the test can see a difference when there is one.
  • What this does not show: that the corpus never matters. If your holdout itself were in it, nothing but the release date would protect you.

A zero-shot comparison you can trust

The final project asks for one — 2 points, for the setup, not for who wins:

  1. Same cutoff, same horizon, same metric as your own model. The foundation model gets the training history as context and nothing after it.
  2. No choosing on the test set. Picking the best of five foundation models by their test score is selection on the test — Week 3’s oldest mistake. Choose on validation windows.
  3. Check the corpus. Chronos: the paper’s Table 3; Chronos-2: Table 6; TimesFM: Wikipedia, Google Trends, M4; TimeGPT: undisclosed. If your data, or a public version of it, is listed, say so.
  4. Check the dates. A model released before your holdout began cannot have seen it.
  5. Report the interval too, and score its coverage (Week 8).
  6. For TimeGPT: your own key, in a Colab secret — never in the notebook you submit.

Wrapping up

Eight ways to get this wrong

Trap What you see Fix
A test window inside the pretraining corpus Results that look too good Corpus lists, release dates, data newer than the model
Choosing the foundation model on the test An optimistic winner Validation windows, then one test
A model used outside its frequencies Lag-Llama on monthly data Check what it was trained on; fine-tune or choose another
Covariates whose future is not known A forecast that cannot be made in practice Only truly known covariates, or forecast them first
Fine-tuning taken as an improvement Sometimes it is worse Score it like any candidate
An API key in a notebook A leaked key, a spent quota Environment variable or Colab secret
Company data sent to an API A privacy problem Ask first; an open model runs locally
“Foundation” read as “better” Losing to ARIMA on your series One more candidate on the rolling origin

What this lecture is, in one slide

  • A foundation model is a model trained once on large, diverse data and used on many tasks — here, any series, zero-shot. Almost always a transformer: tokens, positional encoding, attention.
  • Pretraining → transfer → fine-tuning, done in miniature with N-BEATS on M3, works — and is limited by one horizon, one frequency, one corpus. The real models fix those with generation, patches and billions of observations.
  • TimeGPT (API, conformal intervals, covariates), Lag-Llama (open, Student-\(t\), sub-daily only), Chronos (a language model on binned values), Moirai (any-variate, mixture output), TimesFM (point forecasts, long output patches) differ in architecture, output, covariates and access.
  • On our four stores they forecast well with no fitting; how they compare with ARIMA, and with each other, is a question for your data and your holdout — never settled by a benchmark table.
