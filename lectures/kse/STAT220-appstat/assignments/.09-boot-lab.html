1 Objective

Every lab in this course took one crutch away. Lab 4 gave you an A/B dataset and asked for a decision. Lab 5 took away blind faith in the criterion — you learned to validate it with Monte Carlo. Lab 6 took away “the data is probably normal”. Lab 7 took away the tidy chi-square line. Lab 8 took away the mean. Lab 9 takes away the last one: someone else’s decision layer. This time nobody hands you the threshold, the test, or the verdict template. You are the lead analyst, and the memo that ships or kills the feature carries your signature.

ImportantThis is the course finale — it is worth 16 points (double weight)

Lab 9 is the capstone: it uses every habit the course built — shape forensics, building your own tool step by step, Monte-Carlo validation, design-before-launch, and the decision layer. Budget your time accordingly: this is two labs’ worth of work and two labs’ worth of points.

The story. The “Free delivery on orders over 300 UAH” feature you met in Practice 9 performed well in Kyiv, and GreenBowl is now rolling it out city by city. You have been handed the two-week A/B experiment for your own city (pulled live from greenbowl_boot.py, keyed to your student id). For every user the platform logged their total revenue over the two weeks (2-week ARPU, UAH) — a zero for everyone who ordered nothing, a long right tail for the users who ordered a lot.

Finance did the math once for the whole rollout: the delivery subsidy costs ≈3% of revenue, so in every city the feature pays off only if it lifts ARPU by more than +3% — the breakeven. Experiments across the rollout are planned to reliably catch a +5% effect — the design-MDE. The CFO reads one line per city: “by how many percent did revenue grow, and does the feature pay for itself?”

The platform’s automated report answers a different question. Its headline is a Welch t-test against zero plus a “percent CI” made by dividing the UAH-difference CI by the frozen control mean — the exact two traps Practice 9 exposed: significant ≠ profitable, and the denominator is a random variable too. Your job is to put that headline on trial, rebuild the honest pipeline from the practice — uplift_ci → uplift_ci_pois → rate_uplift → power_sim → ab_report — and deliver the memo the CFO can actually act on.

You will:

  1. Pull your experiment and print the platform’s naive headline — the verdict you are about to put on trial.
  2. Run revenue forensics: zeros, skew, and whether a handful of users holds a suspicious share of an arm’s revenue.
  3. Build the honest uplift engine — the ratio computed whole inside every bootstrap iteration — and compare it with the naive %-CI on your own data.
  4. Rebuild it as a Poisson (weighted) engine — the way industry ships the bootstrap to production — prove the two engines agree, time them honestly, and then compute a country-scale readout that the classic engine cannot touch at any speed.
  5. Earn the right to trust your engine: a Monte-Carlo A/A bench on data shaped like yours, plus the power your sample size actually had.
  6. Assemble the decision layer: three zones against breakeven, P(profitable), and expected value in UAH per year.
  7. Design the next step your verdict demands — extra weeks, a relaunch size, or a robustness statement — by simulation, never by formulaless hope.
  8. Sign a one-page go/no-go memo to a non-technical CFO.

2 General Requirements

ImportantThe fixed business context (same for everyone)
  • Unit: one user.
  • Metric: 2-week ARPU, UAH — total revenue per user over the two weeks, zeros included; higher = better.
  • Arms: control = old delivery pricing, test = free delivery over 300 UAH.
  • Breakeven: the subsidy costs ~3% of revenue → the feature pays off only if ARPU uplift > +3% (BREAKEVEN). This is the decision threshold — the CI is compared to it after the experiment.
  • Design-MDE: rollout experiments are sized to reliably catch +5% (DESIGN_MDE). This is the planning threshold — it sets \(n\) before the experiment. Do not confuse the two (Practice 9, Part IV).
  • Scale: exp.user_base is your city’s user base; exp.annual_revenue = user base × control ARPU × 26 — the number that turns a percent into money.
  • Significance level: \(\alpha = 0.05\).
ImportantEveryone’s experiment is different — on purpose

Your experiment is unique to your id. It might be a clean profitable win, a feature that actively burns money, a result stuck between “significant” and “profitable”, a country-scale sample where significance is guaranteed and worthless, an “uplift” manufactured by a handful of enormous accounts, or an experiment too small to answer the question it was asked. There is no single “right answer” shared across the class — there is only the memo your pipeline supports. Copying another student’s conclusion will be obviously wrong for your city.

3.1 Get Your Experiment

Download greenbowl_boot.py (provided with the lab) into your working folder and load your experiment. Use one id consistently — your KSE email is recommended.

from greenbowl_boot import load_my_experiment, ALPHA, BREAKEVEN, DESIGN_MDE
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import ttest_ind
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_confint

STUDENT_ID = "your.email@kse.org.ua"   # <-- put YOUR id here
exp = load_my_experiment(STUDENT_ID)

print(exp)                     # city, arm sizes, user base
print(exp.data.head())         # one row per user: user_id, arm, revenue
control, test = exp.control, exp.test
print(f"annual revenue at stake: {exp.annual_revenue/1e6:.0f} M UAH")

Then run the platform headline — the automated report every city manager sees. This is the verdict you are about to put on trial; do not believe it yet.

# --- the platform's automated headline (as-is, warts and all) -------------
t_stat, p = ttest_ind(test, control, equal_var=False)
d_hat = test.mean() - control.mean()
se = np.sqrt(test.var(ddof=1)/test.size + control.var(ddof=1)/control.size)
naive = (d_hat - 1.96*se) / control.mean(), (d_hat + 1.96*se) / control.mean()

print(f"ARPU uplift: {d_hat/control.mean():+.2%}   "
      f"'95% CI': ({naive[0]:+.2%}, {naive[1]:+.2%})   p = {p:.3g}")
print("HEADLINE:",
      "SHIP — significant uplift" if p < ALPHA and d_hat > 0 else
      "ROLL BACK — significant drop" if p < ALPHA else
      "DON'T SHIP — no significant uplift")

  2. Run the headline and write down its verdict verbatim.
  3. In two sentences, name the two statistical sins the headline commits even before you look at the data (hint: what is it compared against, and what does it pretend to know exactly?).

3.2 Revenue Forensics

Before trusting any engine, look at what the metric is made of. Revenue data has two standard pathologies: a mass of zeros + a long right tail (which you expect and the bootstrap handles), and concentration — a few users so large that the arm’s mean belongs to them, not to the city (which no resampling trick can fix: the CI’s promise rests on \(\widehat F \approx F\), and a tail your sample barely touches breaks it — the lecture’s edge-statistic warning in A/B costume).

  1. Plot both arms’ revenue distributions (overlaid histograms; a log-x or a “positives only” view is fine as a companion). Report per arm: zero share, mean, median, P90, max.
  2. Concentration check: for each arm compute the share of the arm’s total revenue held by its top-1 and top-5 users. In a healthy arm of thousands of users these are on the order of a fraction of a percent to ~1–2%. Report all four numbers.
  3. In one sentence: given this shape, what could make a mean-based percent verdict lie for your data — and which robust summaries (median uplift, winsorised mean, share of payers) would you check against it?
TipResearch hook

Most students will find an unremarkable concentration profile — and that finding is worth one sentence, not zero: “top-5 users hold X% of each arm, the mean is not hostage to anyone.” For a few of you this check will become the heart of the entire lab. Skipping it and shipping a verdict you cannot defend is the single most expensive mistake here.

3.3 The Honest Engine: uplift_ci

The CFO’s question is relative: \(\text{uplift} = \theta_T/\theta_C - 1\), a statistic where both the numerator and the denominator are random. There is no textbook CI for it — but you own the universal recipe: compute the statistic as a whole on every pair of bootstrap resamples (drawn within each arm, independently), and assemble the central CI \(\big(2\widehat\theta - \theta^*_{[1-\alpha/2]},\, 2\widehat\theta - \theta^*_{[\alpha/2]}\big)\).

  1. Implement Version 1, uplift_ci(control, test, stat=np.mean, alpha=0.05, B=4000, seed=...), returning the point uplift, the central CI, and the array of bootstrap uplifts (star) — you will need the whole distribution later. Resample within each arm; compute the ratio inside every iteration.
  2. Run it on your data and plot the bootstrap distribution of uplift\(^*\) with three vertical marks: zero, BREAKEVEN, and your CI. Describe the distribution’s shape in one sentence — smooth and symmetric, skewed, or lumpy/multimodal — and say what a lumpy shape would tell you about your data (connect to Section 3.2).
  3. Put the honest CI next to the headline’s naive %-CI from Section 3.1. If they roughly agree, say why the small-effect regime makes the naive version lucky (Practice 9, Part I). If they disagree — especially at the lower bound, the line the business reads — explain mechanically which of the headline’s two sins did the damage.
  4. A/A sanity check: split your control arm in half at random (fresh seed), run uplift_ci on the two halves, and confirm the CI covers zero. One line: why is this a necessary-but-not-sufficient check?

3.4 The Industrial Engine: uplift_ci_pois

GreenBowl’s data platform computes metrics in a sharded warehouse — and while your city ran its two-week test, the same feature ran as a parallel country-wide experiment whose log no laptop can analyse the classic way. Rewrite the engine with Poisson weights: every user gets an independent weight \(W_i \sim \text{Pois}(1)\), and the weighted mean \((w \cdot x)/\sum w\) is computed from sums only — one pass over the data, no shared state between rows.

  1. Implement Version 2, uplift_ci_pois(control, test, alpha=0.05, B=4000, seed=...), reusing the same central-CI assembly. Report the share of weights \(W = 0\) next to the number the lecture predicts for it.
  2. Show that the Poisson CI and the classic CI from Section 3.3 practically coincide on your data (or on a fixed-seed subsample if your city is too large for the classic matrix — do the arithmetic from item 4 of the country readout first).
  3. Time both engines honestly on the same data, same \(B\), same machine (time.perf_counter, a couple of repeats). Report both wall-clocks and answer plainly: is the Poisson engine faster here? (Expect: no — drawing \(B \times N\) Poisson weights is no cheaper than drawing \(B \times N\) indices.) One sentence: if it buys no speed on a laptop, what exactly are we buying it for? Your answer is the next sub-section.
ImportantPoisson is not a speed trick

On one machine, over data that fits in memory, the Poisson bootstrap is not faster than the classic one — your own timer just proved it. Its value is structural: the statistic decomposes into per-row sums, so it needs (a) O(B) memory — accumulators instead of a \(B \times N\) matrix, (b) one pass — no going back to the data, and (c) no coordination between rows — every shard of a distributed store can weight its own rows independently. The classic engine needs global random access to all rows at once, which is exactly what a sharded warehouse does not offer. You are about to feel all three.

3.4.1 The country readout: uplift_ci_stream

The national experiment is served by the warehouse API — one shard at a time, per its contract (see the CountryStream docstring):

from greenbowl_boot import load_country_stream

stream = load_country_stream(STUDENT_ID)
print(stream)                    # ~24 shards, ~1.2M users per arm in total
c0, t0 = stream.shard(0)         # one shard: ~50k per arm
WarningThe warehouse contract

Your code may hold one shard plus O(B) accumulators at any moment — never the concatenated dataset. Pretend each shard sits on its own machine: at true warehouse scale even the raw concatenation would not fit, and the export API simply does not offer it. Concatenating the shards into one array voids this section.

  1. Arithmetic first, allocation never. At the country’s \(N\) and your city’s \(B = 4000\): how many GB is the classic \((B, N)\) resample matrix per arm? And even without that matrix — why does classic resampling (rng.choice over all users) structurally require what the warehouse refuses to export? Conclude in one sentence why the classic engine has no path here at any speed.
  2. Implement Version 2b, uplift_ci_stream(stream, alpha=0.05, B=800, seed=...) — one pass over the shards. For each shard \(s\), draw its Poisson weights from a generator seeded by (seed, s) (industry does hash(user_id, b); per-shard seeding is the same idea in miniature) and update four length-\(B\) accumulators: \(S_T[b] \mathrel{+}= w \cdot t\), \(W_T[b] \mathrel{+}= \sum w\), and likewise for control. After the pass: \(\text{uplift}^*_b = (S_T[b]/W_T[b])\,/\,(S_C[b]/W_C[b]) - 1\), then the same central-CI assembly you already own. State your peak memory in words: one shard + four arrays of length \(B\).
  3. Run it on the country. Report the national uplift, its 95% CI, the wall-clock, and the throughput in rows/second — next to your city timings from item 3 above: how many times more data did you just process, and in what memory footprint? One sentence reading the national CI against BREAKEVEN (the decision drama stays with your city — this is the engineering readout HQ asked for).
  4. The shard-independence demo. Process the shards in reverse order (for i in reversed(range(stream.n_shards))) and show the CI is identical in every printed digit. Explain why: each shard’s weights depend only on (seed, shard), so any shard can be processed on any machine, in any order, and the reducer just adds sums. (If you diff the raw floats you will find a residue around the 13th significant digit — that is the order of float additions, not statistics; saying so is worth a sentence.) And one more sentence: why can the classic bootstrap not even state this order-independence property?
  5. To an engineer colleague, one paragraph: why does your uplift_ci_stream translate line-for-line into a SQL GROUP BY over shards? And which statistics does the one-pass trick not cover (Practice 9’s limitation), and what does industry use instead?

3.5 Earn the Right to Trust It: rate_uplift

You deviated from the lecture’s straight path twice — a ratio statistic and Poisson weights — and the course-long habit applies: no criterion is trusted until Monte Carlo says it keeps its promise on data shaped like yours, at your \(n\). In Practice 9 the bench generated worlds from the known ARPU law. You do not know your city’s law — so fit a generator to your control arm: a zero with probability equal to your observed zero share, and a lognormal fitted to the positive revenues (match the median and the log-scale spread). That is the plug-in principle doing double duty.

  1. Build the generator city_world(n, rng, uplift=0.0) fitted to your control arm as described. Show one overlaid histogram (your control vs one simulated sample) as a visual goodness check — after Lab 6 you know better than to skip it.
  2. Implement Version 3, rate_uplift(engine, n, uplift=0.0, ...) — your own loop: simulate A/A worlds at your arm sizes, count how often the engine’s CI excludes zero, report the FPR with a Wilson CI. Run it for uplift_ci_pois. Is \(\alpha\) inside?
  3. Same bench with uplift = DESIGN_MDE: what power did your experiment have, at its actual size, against the effect the rollout was designed to catch? Report it with a Wilson CI.
  4. One sentence interpreting item 3 for your verdict: if your CI turns out wide or your result “not significant”, is that surprising — or exactly what this power figure predicts?
ImportantMatch the bench to your data

A generic copy of the practice’s bench (its ARPU constants, its n = 1500) validates someone else’s experiment. The FPR must be measured at your arm sizes on a generator that mimics your control — zeros and tail included. If your forensics found extreme concentration, say honestly whether your fitted lognormal reproduces it, and what that means for the bench’s verdict.

3.6 The Decision Layer: ab_report

Significance against zero is the wrong threshold: money is compared to breakeven. And a binary “above/below” throws away half the information. Assemble the final tool exactly as the practice built it: three zones against BREAKEVEN (SHIP if the whole CI is above it, KILL if the whole CI is below it, GREY ZONE otherwise), the probability of profitability \(P(\text{uplift}^* \ge \text{breakeven})\) read off the bootstrap distribution, and the expected value \(EV = \text{annual\_revenue} \times (\overline{\text{uplift}^*} - \text{breakeven})\).

  1. Implement Version 4, ab_report(control, test, breakeven, annual_revenue, ...), reusing uplift_ci_pois. It must print: uplift, CI, the three-zone decision, P(profitable), and EV per year in UAH — and return them.
  2. Run it on your experiment with BREAKEVEN and exp.annual_revenue. Read the report as a whole in 2–3 sentences: not just the zone, but what P(profitable) and EV add to it.
  3. Sensitivity to the economics. Finance’s 3% subsidy estimate carries uncertainty. Re-run at breakeven +2% and +4% (one percentage point either side) and state whether your zone survives. A decision that flips inside finance’s error bars is a different memo than one that doesn’t — say which you hold.
  4. Robustness gate. If — and only if — your Section 3.2 concentration check found that a handful of users holds an outsized share of an arm’s revenue, the mean-based report above is not allowed to stand alone. Add: (a) the uplift of the median (via uplift_ci with stat=np.median); (b) the mean uplift with both arms winsorised at their own P99; (c) the mean uplift with the top handful of users removed, stating how many you removed. If the three robust reads and the mean disagree, the story of your lab is why — and your memo’s verdict must follow the read you can defend, not the largest number.
ImportantStop-the-line rule

If your report compares the CI to zero and prints “ship” — everything downstream is void. Zero is the threshold for “does an effect exist”; breakeven is the threshold for “should we pay for it”. The course has shown you enough experiments that are real-but-worthless and grey-but-valuable; the final memo must not confuse the two.

3.7 Design the Next Step: power_sim

A verdict is not a memo until it says what happens next — and “run it a bit longer” is a gut feeling, not a plan. The tool: power by simulation on a pilot. Resample your own data as the “world”, inject a known uplift, and measure how often your engine catches it.

  1. Implement Version 5, power_sim(pilot, uplift, n, ...), reusing uplift_ci_pois, with your control arm as the pilot.
  2. Follow the branch your Section 3.6 zone dictates — and answer that branch’s question with numbers from power_sim:
    • GREY ZONE → how many users per arm does a decisive experiment need (the CI must clear or exclude breakeven), and how many extra weeks is that at your city’s enrollment rate? Assume the experiment enrolls new users at the two-week rate implied by your current arm sizes.
    • CI too wide to say anything (covers zero and breakeven with room to spare) → what a-priori power did this launch have at DESIGN_MDE (you already have it from Section 3.5 — quote it), what \(n\) per arm would a relaunch need for 80% power, and should the original launch have happened at all?
    • Clear SHIP or clear KILL → your decision looks safe; prove it is not fragile. What is the smallest uplift your actual \(n\) catches with ~80% power (the MDE at your n)? Is your observed effect comfortably inside that detection range, and does the zone survive the Section 3.6 sensitivity check?
  3. Whatever the branch: state the cost of your recommendation in plain units — weeks of experimentation, users exposed, or UAH of expected value forgone by waiting.
WarningPost-hoc power is banned by name

Any line of the form “the power at our observed effect was only X%, so the effect is probably real” voids this section. As Practice 9 proved, “power at the observed effect” is a deterministic function of the p-value — zero new information, dressed as an excuse. Power is computed before a launch (or for the next one) on a pilot, at a design effect size — never on the observed result.

3.8 The Final Memo

Write a single-page memo (≤ 400 words) to the non-technical CFO who has only seen the platform’s headline. It must contain:

  1. The headline verdict (quoted) and your one-line sign-off — one of: ship — profitability is proven; kill — the feature loses money; kill — the effect is real but cannot pay for its subsidy; extend — the effect is real, profitability is unproven; here is exactly how much more data decides it; don’t trust the ARPU verdict — the “uplift” belongs to a handful of accounts; here is the metric to use instead; or no conclusion — this experiment could never answer the question; here is the relaunch that can.
  2. The evidence that forces it, quoted as numbers, not adjectives: the honest uplift and CI against breakeven (not zero), P(profitable), EV in UAH per year, the FPR/power of your engine at your \(n\), the robust reads where relevant, and the power_sim sizing of whatever you recommend next.
  3. A concrete recommendation with a price tag: what to do, how long it takes or what it costs, and what number will settle it.
  4. One honest caveat of your own audit (e.g. the bench’s generator is a fit, not the truth; a winsorised metric answers a slightly different question; simulation noise at your n_exps; the EV line assumes the two-week effect persists for a year).

It must be readable by someone who has never heard the words “bootstrap” or “p-value”. You are graded on whether the CFO could sign it without follow-up questions.

Defense

During the defense (5–7 minutes) you will be asked “What if?” questions that probe whether you understand why each piece exists, for example:

  • “Your naive and honest lower bounds differ by two percentage points. Show me — in your own bootstrap code — where the denominator’s randomness enters, and what freezing it would delete.”
  • “Your CI straddles breakeven and P(profitable) is 84%. The CFO says ‘so it’s probably fine — ship it.’ What do you answer, and what single number would change your mind?”
  • “Delete the largest user in your test arm, live, and re-run ab_report. What moved, what didn’t, and what does that say about your memo?”
  • “A city manager says: ‘the test was underpowered at the observed +4%, so the effect is surely real, we just couldn’t see it.’ Respond in two sentences.”
  • “Your laptop has 8 GB of RAM and the national log is 40 GB across 24 shards. Walk me through — accumulator by accumulator — how your uplift_ci_stream produces the CI, and what exactly you would ship to each shard’s worker in a real warehouse.”
  • “Your own timer showed the Poisson engine is no faster than the classic one on your city. Why did you still use it for ab_report, and what breaks if the metric is the median basket instead of ARPU?”
  • “Finance revises the subsidy: breakeven is +5%, not +3%. Re-run the report live and give me the new memo in one sentence.”
  • “Your A/A bench shows FPR = 9% with a Wilson CI of (6%, 13%). Which of your report’s numbers does that poison, and what do you do next?”
  • “You recommend four more weeks. What exactly did you simulate to get that number, and why is resampling your own experiment a legitimate stand-in for the city?”

You may also be asked to change one thing live (swap the statistic to the median, winsorise an arm, move breakeven, shrink B, re-seed the bench) and explain the resulting shift.
