Why forecast at all

Every plan is a forecast

Whether or not anyone calls it that, a digital business forecasts every week:

Decision The forecast behind it
Next month’s ad budget installs, CPI, conversion
Servers, support shifts traffic, tickets by hour
Revenue plan for the board subscriptions, renewals, churn
When a campaign pays back the cohort’s revenue curve
An alert on a dashboard “what is normal today?”

A useful forecast has four parts:

  1. A number — the most likely value.
  2. A range — how wrong it can reasonably be.
  3. A horizon — how far ahead it is honest.
  4. A decision it feeds. A forecast nobody acts on is a chart.

Today: how to get all four — and whether the new AI models get them better than the old tools.

What is new in 2026

You already use foundation models for text: one model, trained on most of the internet, answers questions about documents it has never seen.

Since 2023–24 the same idea exists for time series: models trained on hundreds of billions of data points — web traffic, energy, retail, weather — that forecast your metric with no training at all. Amazon, Google, Salesforce and Nixtla release them; most are free.

Three questions for today:

  • Do they work on the kind of numbers you look at — product attention, sales, traffic?
  • Where do they fail, and how do you notice before the board does?
  • Can’t I just paste the numbers into ChatGPT or Claude? We will measure it.

Our data today

Daily Wikipedia page views of real products and events — ChatGPT, Duolingo, Spotify, Netflix, Tinder, Strava, TikTok, Notion, Threads, Black Friday. A public, honest proxy for attention to a product, with the same habits as your dashboards: weekly rhythms, seasons, campaigns, launches, decline.

  • Weekly season: Tuesday and Wednesday are about 16% above an average day, Saturday about 22% below — the rhythm of work and study. Any forecast that misses it is wrong every weekend.
  • Trend: after the weekly rhythm is removed, what remains moves slowly — that is what a forecast extends.
  • What is left is mostly small noise, with a few huge days: events no history explains.

These are the benchmarks. A model — any model, however clever — earns its place only by beating them on data it has not seen. Surprisingly often, it does not.

Judging a forecast honestly: backtesting

Code 
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(12, 2.0))
for k in range(4):
    y = 3 - k
    ax.add_patch(plt.Rectangle((0, y), 6 + k, 0.6, color=blue, alpha=0.6))
    ax.add_patch(plt.Rectangle((6 + k, y), 1, 0.6, color=red_pink, alpha=0.8))
    ax.text(-0.2, y + 0.3, f"window {k + 1}", ha="right", va="center", fontsize=13)
ax.text(3, 4.0, "history the model may use", color=blue, ha="center", fontsize=13)
ax.text(10, 4.0, "the period to forecast", color=red_pink, ha="center", fontsize=13)
ax.set_xlim(-2, 12)
ax.set_ylim(-0.2, 4.4)
ax.axis("off")
plt.show()
  • Cut the history at a past date, forecast the next weeks, compare with what actually happened. Move the cut forward and repeat: one window is luck, six windows are evidence.
  • Never judge a model on the data it was fitted to, and never shuffle days at random — the future must stay in the future.
  • Score with an error you can explain: MAE (average miss, in views), MAPE (average miss, in %).

For analysts

MAPE explodes near zero and rewards under-forecasting; compare models with a scaled error instead. We use MASE: the model’s MAE divided by the MAE of “same day last week” in the past. Below 1 — better than that rule.

A range, not a number

chronos2 = BaseChronosPipeline.from_pretrained("amazon/chronos-2", device_map="cpu")

Days inside the P10–P90 band: 64% (a calibrated band holds about 80%)
  • P50: half the days will be above, half below. P10 / P90: a bad case and a good case.
  • Budget to P50, keep the cash for P90, check that P10 does not break anything.
  • The band is only worth something if it is calibrated: count how often reality falls inside.

The classical toolbox, in one slide

Method Idea Good at Weak at
Seasonal naive repeat last week / last year strong seasons everything else
Exponential smoothing (ETS) recent past weighs more level, trend, one season events, several seasons
ARIMA regression on its own past short-term dynamics long horizons, regime changes
Prophet trend + seasons + holidays readable, quick no memory of yesterday
Gradient boosting (LightGBM) features → number, across many series many similar series needs feature work

All of them are fitted to your series: someone chooses the model, its settings, its features, and refits it as data arrive. That is the work a foundation model promises to remove.

Foundation models for time series

  • Pretraining: a transformer — the architecture behind ChatGPT — learns from a huge, varied pile of series what usually comes next.
  • Zero-shot: you give it your history; it gives back a forecast. No fitting, no model choice.
  • Tokens: a language model reads words; these read values or short patches of values. Chronos, for example, scales the series and turns each value into one of 4096 “words”.

Who makes them

Model From Released Access Covariates Note
Chronos-2 Amazon Oct 2025 free, runs on a laptop yes strong across public benchmarks
TimesFM 2.5 Google Sep 2025 free, runs on a laptop yes (linear add-on) trained mostly on Wikipedia page views
TimeGPT Nixtla 2023– paid API, 30-day trial yes your data goes to their servers
Moirai 2.0 Salesforce 2025 free yes its first version was trained on the open LOTSA corpus
TiRex NX-AI 2025 free, small (35M) no a recurrent (xLSTM) design

Open models run on your own machine — the data never leave it. API models need an account, cost money at scale, and send your numbers to someone else’s server: ask before you upload company data.

Zero-shot in four lines

from chronos import BaseChronosPipeline
import torch

model = BaseChronosPipeline.from_pretrained("amazon/chronos-2", device_map="cpu")   # ~500 MB, once
quantiles, mean = model.predict_quantiles([torch.tensor(history)], prediction_length=28,
                                          quantile_levels=[0.1, 0.5, 0.9])

That is the whole of it: no seasonal period declared, no parameters chosen, no training. These four lines drew the P10–P90 range on the ChatGPT chart earlier.

The question is not whether it runs. It is whether it beats “same day last week” — on many products, on many weeks, on data the model cannot have seen.

The test: ten products, six months

  • Ten daily series, from ChatGPT to Black Friday.
  • Six windows of 28 days, May to October 2026 — all after the release of every model tested, so none of them can have seen the answers.
  • Six forecasters: three classical rules and models, three foundation models. The same history for each.
The backtest, in code 
import timesfm
import tirex

products = ["ChatGPT", "Duolingo", "Spotify", "Netflix", "Tinder", "Strava", "TikTok", "Notion",
            "Threads", "Black Friday"]
panel = views[views["unique_id"].isin(products) & (views["ds"] >= "2023-01-01")].dropna()
H, WINDOWS = 28, 6
dates = np.sort(panel["ds"].unique())
cutoffs = [dates[len(dates) - (WINDOWS - k) * H - 1] for k in range(WINDOWS)]

tfm = timesfm.TimesFM_2p5_200M_torch.from_pretrained("google/timesfm-2.5-200m-pytorch")
tfm.compile(timesfm.ForecastConfig(max_context=1024, max_horizon=128, normalize_inputs=True,
                                   use_continuous_quantile_head=True))
tirex_model = tirex.load_model("NX-AI/TiRex", device="cpu")

frames = []
for cutoff in cutoffs:
    history = panel[panel["ds"] <= cutoff]
    window = panel[panel["ds"] > cutoff].groupby("unique_id").head(H).copy()
    classic = StatsForecast(models=[Naive(alias="Naive"), SeasonalNaive(7, alias="Seasonal naive"),
                                    AutoETS(season_length=7, alias="ETS")], freq="D")
    window = window.merge(classic.forecast(df=history[history["ds"] > cutoff - np.timedelta64(730, "D")], h=H),
                          on=["unique_id", "ds"])
    ids = sorted(history["unique_id"].unique())
    contexts = [torch.tensor(history[history["unique_id"] == i]["y"].values, dtype=torch.float32) for i in ids]
    keys = pd.DataFrame({"unique_id": np.repeat(ids, H), "ds": np.tile(np.sort(window["ds"].unique()), len(ids))})
    q, _ = chronos2.predict_quantiles(contexts, prediction_length=H, quantile_levels=[0.1, 0.5, 0.9])
    keys["Chronos-2"] = np.concatenate([np.asarray(x).reshape(-1, 3)[:, 1] for x in q])
    keys["Chronos-2 P10"] = np.concatenate([np.asarray(x).reshape(-1, 3)[:, 0] for x in q])
    keys["Chronos-2 P90"] = np.concatenate([np.asarray(x).reshape(-1, 3)[:, 2] for x in q])
    point, _ = tfm.forecast(horizon=H, inputs=[c.numpy() for c in contexts])
    keys["TimesFM"] = np.asarray(point).ravel()
    _, mean = tirex_model.forecast(context=contexts, prediction_length=H)
    keys["TiRex"] = np.asarray(mean).ravel()
    frames.append(window.merge(keys, on=["unique_id", "ds"]).assign(cutoff=cutoff))
backtest = pd.concat(frames)

scale = {}
for name in products:                                   # MAE of "same day last week", in the year before
    v = panel[(panel["unique_id"] == name) & (panel["ds"] <= cutoffs[0])]["y"].values[-365:]
    scale[name] = np.mean(np.abs(v[7:] - v[:-7]))
forecasters = ["Naive", "Seasonal naive", "ETS", "Chronos-2", "TimesFM", "TiRex"]
mase = backtest.groupby("unique_id").apply(
    lambda g: pd.Series({m: np.mean(np.abs(g["y"] - g[m])) / scale[g.name] for m in forecasters}),
    include_groups=False)

Events: the part that matters most

A predictable peak: Black Friday

If you know the date, tell the model

A covariate is something you know about the future: a release date, a promo calendar, a holiday, a price change. Chronos-2 can take it. Example: Lionel Messi’s page views during the 2026 World Cup, forecast on 10 June 2026 — once from history only, once with a 0/1 “tournament day” flag.

Code 
messi = views[views["unique_id"] == "Lionel Messi"][["ds", "y"]].assign(item_id="messi")
messi["y"] = np.log(messi["y"])
tournaments = [("2016-06-03", 23), ("2018-06-14", 31), ("2019-06-14", 23), ("2021-06-13", 27),
               ("2022-11-20", 28), ("2024-06-20", 24), ("2026-06-11", 38)]
messi["tournament"] = 0
for begin, days in tournaments:
    messi.loc[messi["ds"].between(begin, pd.Timestamp(begin) + pd.Timedelta(days=days)), "tournament"] = 1
before = messi[messi["ds"] < "2026-06-11"]
after = messi[messi["ds"] >= "2026-06-11"].head(51)
kw = dict(id_column="item_id", timestamp_column="ds", target="y", prediction_length=51, quantile_levels=[0.5])
alone = chronos2.predict_df(before[["item_id", "ds", "y"]], **kw)
flagged = chronos2.predict_df(before[["item_id", "ds", "y", "tournament"]],
                              future_df=after[["item_id", "ds", "tournament"]], **kw)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(12, 3.0))
recent = messi[messi["ds"].between("2026-04-15", after["ds"].max())]
ax.plot(recent["ds"], np.exp(recent["y"]), color=slate, label="actual")
ax.plot(after["ds"], np.exp(alone["predictions"]), color=blue, linewidth=1.8, label="history only")
ax.plot(after["ds"], np.exp(flagged["predictions"]), color=red_pink, linewidth=1.8, label="+ tournament flag")
ax.axvspan(pd.Timestamp("2026-06-11"), pd.Timestamp("2026-07-19"), color=orange, alpha=0.08)
ax.set_yscale("log")
ax.legend(loc="upper left", fontsize=8)
plt.show()
y_wc = after["y"].values
print(f"Average miss (log points): history only {np.mean(np.abs(y_wc - alone['predictions'])):.2f}, "
      f"with the flag {np.mean(np.abs(y_wc - flagged['predictions'])):.2f}")
Average miss (log points): history only 1.31, with the flag 0.87

The flag cut the average miss by about a third — the model learned what a tournament does from the six earlier ones in the history. It still under-shot the peaks. Release dates, promo calendars and holidays are the covariates a product team always has.

A forecast as an alarm

The same range makes a monitor: forecast each day from the days before it; if the day falls outside the model’s 1%–99% range, raise a flag. Netflix, the last 120 days:

Code 
nf = series["Netflix"]
days = nf.index[-120:]
ctxs = [torch.tensor(nf[:d - pd.Timedelta(days=1)].values[-1024:], dtype=torch.float32) for d in days]
qm, _ = chronos2.predict_quantiles(ctxs, prediction_length=1, quantile_levels=[0.01, 0.5, 0.99])
qm = np.stack([np.asarray(x).reshape(-1)[:3] for x in qm])
actual_m = nf[days].values
alarm = (actual_m > qm[:, 2]) | (actual_m < qm[:, 0])
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(12, 3.2))
ax.fill_between(days, qm[:, 0], qm[:, 2], color=turquoise, alpha=0.25, label="1%–99%, forecast the day before")
ax.plot(days, actual_m, color=slate, linewidth=1, label="actual")
ax.scatter(days[alarm], actual_m[alarm], color=red_pink, s=40, zorder=3, label=f"{alarm.sum()} alarms")
ax.yaxis.set_major_formatter(thousands)
ax.legend(loc="upper left", fontsize=8)
plt.show()

Three alarms in 120 days: few enough to read every one. The band is wide in July, after a spike, and narrow in quiet weeks — the monitor adapts to how noisy the metric has just been, which a fixed “±20%” rule cannot.

Where foundation models fail

What the backtests and the cases say, together:

  • New events of unknown size — a campaign, a viral moment, an outage. No model has seen this one. Plan scenarios, not a number.
  • Breaks in the series — a tracking change, a redesigned funnel, a new market. The model extends a past that no longer applies. Look at the chart before you forecast it.
  • Very short histories — a product launched last week. Better than nothing; a range wide enough to say so.
  • Data it may already have seen. TimesFM was trained mostly on Wikipedia page views. Our test windows are after its release, so it could not have seen them — but a backtest on old public data can flatter any foundation model.

For analysts

A test of memorisation on the M4 competition data — which is in Chronos’s training corpus — found no advantage on the windows it had seen (MATH840, week 10). Absence of evidence on one corpus is not a guarantee on yours: prefer test periods after the model’s release date.

Why a chat is the wrong tool for the numbers

  • Numbers are text to a language model. It writes digits one token at a time; it was trained to sound plausible, not to be calibrated. It can return 36 numbers when asked for 28.
  • One answer, no range. You get a single path, not P10–P90 — and ask again, and you may get a different one.
  • You cannot backtest a conversation. The honest test above needed 60 runs and a script.
  • Privacy. Pasting company metrics into a public chat is a data decision, not a convenience.

A large chat model writes better numbers than a 1.5-billion-parameter one. None of the four points changes.

The right division of labour

The AI assistant / agent does the work around the numbers:

  • writes the data pull and the backtest script;
  • runs the foundation model and the baselines;
  • builds the chart and the weekly report;
  • explains the result in plain language.

The foundation model produces the numbers and the range.

The backtest decides whether to trust them.

A prompt that works:

I have a CSV with columns date, value (daily
installs, 2 years). Write a Python notebook that:
1. holds out the last 4 weeks, in 6 rolling windows;
2. forecasts them with "same day last week" and with
   Chronos-2 (amazon/chronos-2), P10/P50/P90;
3. reports MASE for both and how often the actual
   value falls inside P10–P90;
4. then forecasts the next 4 weeks with the winner.
Do not tune anything on the hold-out weeks.

A checklist for any AI-made forecast

Before a forecast goes into a plan, ask — or ask your agent to show:

  1. What does it beat? “Same day last week” and “same day last year”, on held-out weeks.
  2. How many windows? One is luck. Several, in different weeks, are evidence.
  3. Is the range calibrated? About 80% of days inside P10–P90 — counted, not assumed.
  4. What is the horizon? Accuracy at 7 days says nothing about 90.
  5. Did the test data exist before the model was released? If not, say so.
  6. What does it not know? Campaigns, launches, price changes, breaks — and who adds them.
  7. What decision changes if the truth lands at P10 instead of P50?

What to take away

  • A forecast is a number, a range, a horizon and a decision — the range matters as much as the number.
  • Baselines first, backtests always. “Same day last week” is the bar; one lucky window is not evidence.
  • Foundation models are a real step: free, fast, no tuning — and on our ten products they beat the classical rules on almost every one.
  • They do not know your calendar. Predictable seasons they learn from history; campaigns, launches and breaks you must tell them — or plan scenarios around.
  • Language models are for the work around the numbers, not for the numbers. Let the agent write the backtest, let the foundation model forecast, let the backtest decide.
